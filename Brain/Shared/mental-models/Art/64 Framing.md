# Framing

## Description

**Framing** is the mental model describing how the way information is presented — its context, emphasis, language, and comparison points — shapes how it is perceived and acted upon, independently of the underlying content. The same facts presented with different framing produce systematically different decisions. Understanding framing is essential both for constructing effective communication and for defending against manipulation.

- **Origins:** Formally demonstrated by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in their landmark 1981 paper on the "Asian Disease Problem" — the same outcome described as saving 200 lives vs. 400 dying produced opposite majority preferences, violating rational decision theory. This work won Kahneman the Nobel Prize in Economics. Erving Goffman's *Frame Analysis* (1974) earlier established framing as a fundamental sociological concept. George Lakoff extended it to political communication in *Don't Think of an Elephant* (2004).

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Loss vs. gain framing:** People are loss-averse — they respond more strongly to losing $100 than gaining $100. "10% failure rate" is more alarming than "90% success rate." The content is identical; the frame is not.
    - **Reference point dependency:** All evaluation is relative to a reference point. The frame sets the reference point. Change the frame, change the evaluation.
    - **Inclusion and exclusion:** A frame determines not just what is emphasized but what is excluded from consideration. Every frame makes some things visible and others invisible.
    - **Metaphor as frame:** The metaphors we use to describe a problem import an entire frame. "Crime is a beast to be caged" vs. "crime is a disease to be treated" leads to entirely different policy responses (Thibodeau and Boroditsky, 2011).
    - **Frame control:** Whoever sets the frame wins the debate. The first frame introduced is sticky — subsequent arguments are evaluated within it.

- **Real-World Examples:** "90% fat-free" vs. "10% fat" — same yogurt, dramatically different consumer response. "Estate tax" (neutral frame) vs. "death tax" (loaded frame). A performance review framing an employee's directness as "aggressive" vs. "assertive." A startup framing its high burn rate as "aggressive investment in growth."

---

## When to Use

- **Communication and persuasion:** Deliberately choose the frame that most accurately represents your position in terms that resonate with your audience's values.
- **Decision audit:** Before making any important decision, identify the frame being used and ask whether alternative frames reveal different implications.
- **Negotiation:** Control the frame early — whoever defines the terms of debate has a structural advantage.
- **Problem-solving:** Reframing a problem often reveals solutions invisible in the original frame.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Manipulative framing:** Deliberately framing facts in misleading ways is ethically wrong and erodes trust when discovered.
- **Over-analyzing framing:** In time-sensitive decisions, extensive frame analysis can induce paralysis. Apply proportionate to the stakes.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the current frame:** How is the situation, problem, or offer currently being described? What reference points, metaphors, and emphases are being used?
2. **Test the frame:** Does the current frame serve your interests and reflect reality accurately?
3. **Generate alternative frames:** How could this same situation be described differently? What becomes visible with each alternative frame?
4. **Identify the framing's effects:** Is the current frame creating loss aversion, setting an unfavorable anchor, or excluding important considerations?
5. **Reframe deliberately:** Choose the frame that most accurately represents your position and resonates with your audience's values.
6. **Guard against hostile framing:** In adversarial contexts, notice when someone else is setting the frame and explicitly name and reject it if unfavorable.
7. **Test for completeness:** Does your frame make important information invisible? Add what is essential even if it complicates the frame.
