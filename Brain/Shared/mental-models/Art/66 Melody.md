# Melody

## Description

**Melody** is the mental model of the memorable throughline — the core sequence or narrative that carries the meaning of a communication and is what the audience retains. In music, melody is what you hum after the concert. Applied to strategy, leadership, and communication, melody is the central idea, the single message, the through-line that, when clearly established and consistently repeated, makes everything else cohere and stick.

- **Origins:** Musical melody theory is ancient, but the concept as a communication and strategy framework draws from modern cognitive science — research showing that humans have limited working memory, that they remember narratives better than data, and that message clarity requires radical prioritization. Carmine Gallo's analysis of great communicators, McKinsey's "so what" principle, and Chip and Dan Heath's "core idea" in *Made to Stick* all operationalize the melody concept.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **The curse of complexity:** Without a clear melody, communication becomes noise — multiple themes competing for attention, with none landing. Complexity feels thorough but is actually a failure to prioritize.
    - **Repetition cements the melody:** Unlike in music where repetition can become boring, in communication the melody must be stated, restated, and illustrated multiple times from multiple angles before it lodges in the audience's memory.
    - **Everything else is accompaniment:** Supporting data, examples, and sub-arguments are the harmony and rhythm — they only work when they serve the melody, not compete with it.
    - **The single sentence test:** If you cannot state your melody in a single memorable sentence, you don't have one yet. Jeff Bezos required Amazon press releases to begin with the customer headline — the melody.
    - **Strategic melody:** Organizations and leaders need a melody — a consistent, clear narrative about what they stand for and why. Without it, strategy documents, messaging, and culture become fragmented noise.

- **Real-World Examples:** Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" is the melody — every example, contrast, and rhetorical flourish serves it. Apple's "1,000 songs in your pocket" melody for the iPod launch. "Don't be evil" as Google's early organizational melody. A startup's investor pitch with a clear melody: "We are making healthcare affordable for the 500M people who can't afford it."

---

## When to Use

- **Any persuasive communication:** Identify the single most important thing you want your audience to remember and build everything else to serve it.
- **Strategy and vision:** Develop an organizational melody — a clear, memorable statement of purpose and direction — before building supporting detail.
- **Leadership communication:** Repeat the melody consistently across different contexts and audiences. Leaders who are remembered are those who kept returning to their core message.
- **Problem-solving:** Identify the central insight (the melody) before adding supporting analysis. If you can't state the core insight simply, you haven't found it yet.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When nuance is the point:** Some communications must convey genuine complexity without a single melody — legal contracts, technical specifications, balanced policy analyses.
- **When the melody is premature:** Don't force a melody before you've genuinely understood the problem. Sometimes the melody emerges from the analysis, not before it.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify your core message:** What is the single most important thing your audience should understand or believe after this communication?
2. **State it in one sentence:** Craft the melody as a simple, memorable sentence.
3. **Test it for clarity:** Would a stranger understand this sentence without context? Is it specific enough to be useful?
4. **Build the accompaniment:** Arrange supporting points, data, and examples to develop and reinforce the melody, not compete with it.
5. **State the melody explicitly early:** Lead with it, not with background. Audiences who know the melody can organize incoming information against it.
6. **Repeat it:** Return to the melody multiple times — at different moments, in different words, with different illustrations.
7. **End with it:** Your conclusion should return to the melody, now enriched by everything that supported it.
