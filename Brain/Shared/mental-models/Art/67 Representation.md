# Representation

## Description

**Representation** is the mental model recognizing that any model, map, image, language, or description is a representation of reality — not reality itself. Every representation selects some features of a phenomenon to include and others to exclude, highlights certain relationships, and uses a particular medium that shapes what can be expressed. The choice of representation profoundly shapes what can be thought, communicated, and solved.

- **Origins:** Philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein's picture theory of meaning (*Tractatus*, 1921) argued that propositions are representations of states of affairs. Alfred Korzybski's "the map is not the territory" (1931) made the limits of representation a central cognitive principle. In mathematics and computer science, representation theory is a formal discipline exploring how choosing the right data structure determines which algorithms are possible.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Representations are lossy:** Every representation omits something. A map cannot include every feature of the territory; an equation cannot capture all dimensions of a physical phenomenon.
    - **The medium shapes the message:** Different representations of the same content illuminate different aspects and conceal others. A table, a graph, and a narrative tell different stories about the same data.
    - **Representation determines tractability:** The same problem, expressed in different representations, can be easy or impossible to solve. The key insight in many breakthroughs was choosing the right representation (coordinates for geometry, matrices for transformations, networks for relationships).
    - **Representation and thought:** The representations available to you constrain what you can think. A language without a word for a concept makes that concept harder to think about (weakly — the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis). Mathematical notation extends what is cognitively possible.
    - **Switching representations reveals new possibilities:** When stuck, try representing the problem differently — visually, mathematically, narratively, spatially. New representations reveal hidden structure.

- **Real-World Examples:** Florence Nightingale's revolutionary "rose diagram" — a visual representation of hospital mortality data — communicated what dry statistics could not and drove medical reform. Feynman diagrams gave physicists a new representation for quantum electrodynamics that made previously intractable calculations routine. Mapping a business problem as a network graph reveals clustering and bottlenecks invisible in a spreadsheet.

---

## When to Use

- **Problem-solving:** When stuck, explicitly change the representation of the problem — switch from numbers to pictures, from narrative to equations, from linear list to network.
- **Communication:** Choose the representation that best serves the audience — the right chart, diagram, metaphor, or example.
- **Evaluating models and analysis:** Ask what the representation includes, excludes, and distorts. What features of reality does it make invisible?
- **Innovation:** Identify whether an industry is using an outdated representation. New representations (digital vs. physical, networked vs. hierarchical) create new capabilities.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Mistaking the representation for reality:** The most dangerous use of this model is violating it — treating your model as reality rather than a useful but limited map. Stay epistemically humble.
- **Representation for its own sake:** Complex visual representations and elaborate frameworks that obscure rather than illuminate are representations that fail their purpose.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the current representation:** How is the problem, data, or concept currently being expressed?
2. **Audit its inclusions and exclusions:** What does this representation capture well? What does it hide or distort?
3. **Generate alternative representations:** How else could this be expressed — visually, mathematically, narratively, spatially, or through analogy?
4. **Test each representation:** Which one makes the key relationships most visible? Which makes the problem most tractable?
5. **Choose for purpose:** Select the representation that best serves your specific goal (communication, problem-solving, prediction, discovery).
6. **Stay aware of the limits:** Explicitly note what your chosen representation cannot capture.
7. **Switch when stuck:** When analysis stalls, change the representation — new structure reveals new solutions.
