# Performance

## Description

**Performance** is the mental model, drawn from sociologist Erving Goffman's dramaturgical framework, that social life is a performance in which actors manage impressions through their presentation in "front stage" (public) contexts, while their "back stage" (private) reality may differ. Applied broadly, it describes the gap between public presentation and private reality in individuals, organizations, and cultures — and reveals how understanding this gap is essential to accurate judgment.

- **Origins:** Erving Goffman introduced the framework in *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959), arguing that all social interaction involves impression management — we present ourselves strategically to different audiences. The model draws on sociological interactionism and overlaps with psychological research on self-presentation, authenticity, and the gap between espoused values (what people say they believe) and enacted values (what their behavior reveals).

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Front stage vs. back stage:** Front stage is where performance occurs — the deliberate presentation for an audience. Back stage is where the performance is prepared, rested from, and abandoned. The gap between them is where truth lives.
    - **Impression management:** Everyone manages the impression they create — this is not dishonesty but a universal feature of social interaction. The question is whether front-stage and back-stage are aligned or fundamentally contradictory.
    - **Authenticity vs. performance:** Authentic performance aligns front and back stage — you behave consistently whether observed or not. Inauthentic performance creates a large gap that collapses under observation or stress.
    - **Organizational performance:** Companies have front-stage presentations (values statements, PR, customer-facing behavior) and back-stage realities (how employees are actually treated, how decisions are actually made). The gap between them is the measure of organizational integrity.
    - **Strategic use of performance:** Understanding that all communication involves performance enables you to design your front-stage presentation deliberately — not for deception but for effective communication, leadership, and influence.

- **Real-World Examples:** A company's mission statement is front-stage performance; its actual resource allocation decisions reveal the back stage. A job interview is pure front stage — both parties performing idealized versions of themselves. A leader who behaves consistently whether or not they're being observed is demonstrating alignment of front and back stage — the definition of trustworthiness. A politician's town hall is scripted front stage; investigative journalism seeks the back stage.

---

## When to Use

- **Evaluating trustworthiness:** Seek back-stage information — how people behave when not being evaluated, what employees say about the company culture internally.
- **Designing your own presentation:** Understand that your communication is always partly performance — design it intentionally to convey the right message, without crossing into inauthenticity.
- **Organizational diagnosis:** Compare the company's front-stage values to its back-stage decisions. The gap reveals the real culture.
- **Negotiation and influence:** Understand that counterparts are managing impressions — look for back-stage signals (how they treat subordinates, what they do when not negotiating) to calibrate your assessment.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Using it to justify cynicism:** The existence of performance doesn't mean all presentation is manipulation. Many people's front and back stages are highly aligned.
- **Over-analyzing low-stakes interactions:** Not every social exchange requires dramaturgical analysis. Apply it where the stakes and potential gap are significant.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the front stage:** What is the formal, observed presentation in this context — the stated values, the public behavior, the official narrative?
2. **Seek back-stage signals:** What is visible when the observation is reduced or absent? Employee reviews, informal conversations, behavior in low-stakes moments?
3. **Measure the gap:** How large is the distance between front-stage and back-stage? Large gaps signal either deliberate deception or self-delusion.
4. **Identify your own front-stage performance:** What impression are you creating? Is it aligned with your back-stage reality?
5. **Design for authenticity:** Reduce the gap between your front and back stage — align your presented self with your actual values and behavior.
6. **Use performance intentionally:** Where presentation matters (leadership communication, negotiation, public speaking), design your front stage deliberately to serve your genuine goals.
7. **Account for audience in others' back stages:** Everyone has a back stage — if they trust you, you may see it. If they don't, you will only see the performance.
