# Genre

## Description

**Genre** is the mental model describing the implicit contract between creator and audience — a shared set of conventions, expectations, and rules that define what a communication or experience is supposed to do and how it should do it. Violating genre expectations without intent creates confusion; violating them deliberately creates surprise, innovation, or humor.

- **Origins:** Genre theory originated in literary criticism (Aristotle's *Poetics* distinguished tragedy, comedy, and epic) and was formalized in the 20th century by theorists like Mikhail Bakhtin, who argued that all language use is inherently genre-mediated. In everyday application, the model extends to any domain — business meetings have genre conventions, emails have genre conventions, scientific papers have genre conventions.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Genre as a contract:** When you signal a genre (through format, opening, setting, or medium), you make a promise to your audience about what to expect. Honoring the contract creates satisfaction; breaking it accidentally creates frustration.
    - **Genre enables efficient communication:** Because conventions are shared, much information can be conveyed implicitly. A "quarterly earnings call" implies a specific structure, vocabulary, and purpose without anyone saying so.
    - **Genre violations as tools:** Deliberate genre subversion — the thriller that turns into a love story, the business memo written as a poem — can create powerful effects precisely because they violate expectations. But this only works when the violation is intentional and the audience eventually recognizes it.
    - **Genre drift:** Genres evolve over time as conventions are tested, updated, and replaced. Outdated genre conventions signal out-of-touch creators.
    - **Cross-genre innovation:** Some of the most powerful creative and communicative innovations come from combining genres in unexpected ways — the documentary thriller, the memoir-as-business-book, the data-driven journalism piece.

- **Real-World Examples:** A job application that reads like a love letter violates genre and will likely be rejected — not because the content is bad but because it signals a failure to understand the conventions. A TED Talk has a very specific genre: one big idea, personal narrative, universal implication, ~18 minutes. Deviating from it confuses the audience. Quentin Tarantino's films systematically violate genre conventions to create art.

---

## When to Use

- **Any formal communication:** Identify the genre before creating — what does this type of communication conventionally include, exclude, and signal?
- **Diagnosing communication failures:** If a message isn't landing, ask whether a genre mismatch is creating confusion.
- **Innovation and differentiation:** Deliberately combine or subvert genres to stand out, but ensure the violation is intentional and legible.
- **Entering a new professional context:** Learning the genre conventions of a new field, company, or role accelerates integration and reduces social friction.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When the genre itself is the problem:** Sometimes the genre enforces bad habits (overly bureaucratic memos, performative academic writing). Breaking the genre intentionally may be the right solution — but do it consciously.
- **Over-rigidly following genre:** Genre conventions are guides, not laws. Slavish adherence produces derivative, forgettable work.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the genre:** What type of communication is this — email, pitch, report, conversation, presentation?
2. **Identify the genre conventions:** What does the audience expect in terms of format, length, tone, vocabulary, and structure?
3. **Decide: honor or subvert?** Will you follow the conventions (to meet expectations) or deliberately violate them (to create a specific effect)?
4. **If honoring: fulfill the contract:** Ensure the communication delivers what the genre promises.
5. **If subverting: signal the subversion:** Make the violation legible — help the audience understand the departure is intentional.
6. **Check for accidental genre mixing:** Review the communication for unintentional genre signals that might confuse the audience.
7. **Update your genre knowledge:** Genres evolve — ensure your conventions are current, not outdated.
