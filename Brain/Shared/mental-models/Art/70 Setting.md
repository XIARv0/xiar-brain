# Setting

## Description

**Setting** is the mental model recognizing that the environment — physical, social, organizational, temporal — profoundly shapes what actions are possible and what behaviors are likely. People make different decisions in different settings, not because their character changes but because the environment structures incentives, cues, and constraints. Designing the setting is often more powerful than trying to change people.

- **Origins:** In literary theory, setting establishes the context within which character and plot operate — it is not mere backdrop but an active force. In behavioral science, this insight was formalized by Kurt Lewin's field theory (behavior is a function of person AND environment), then operationalized by Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein in *Nudge* (2008) — "choice architecture" describes how the setting of choices shapes decisions without constraining freedom.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Behavior is situation-dependent:** The same person behaves differently in different settings. The Stanford Prison Experiment and Milgram's obedience studies demonstrated how far situational factors override individual character.
    - **Choice architecture:** The physical and informational layout of a decision environment shapes choices — the default option, the placement of items, the framing of choices. Hospital cafeterias that put salads at eye level and desserts at the back change eating behavior without banning anything.
    - **Environmental cues trigger behaviors:** Settings prime associated behaviors automatically. A library primes quiet and focus; a sports bar primes noise and excitement; a cluttered desk primes distraction.
    - **Designing for desired behavior:** To change behavior sustainably, change the setting — the default, the cue, the social norm, the physical environment. Relying on willpower against a hostile environment is expensive and unreliable.
    - **Organizational setting:** Organizational culture is a setting — it determines what is normal, expected, rewarded, and punished. You cannot install culture by decree; you must design the setting that produces it.

- **Real-World Examples:** Google's office food policy — making healthy food the default, visible, and convenient — changed employee nutrition without mandating anything. Open-plan offices (a setting choice) change communication patterns, collaboration, and concentration. A hospital that creates a "stop the line" culture (setting) reduces surgical errors more than training individual surgeons.

---

## When to Use

- **Behavior change:** Before trying to change people through willpower or incentives, ask whether changing the setting would produce the desired behavior automatically.
- **Organizational design:** Design the physical, informational, and social environment to produce the culture and behavior you want.
- **Decision quality improvement:** Remove decision-making from adverse settings (tired, stressed, distracted) and move it to settings that support clear thinking.
- **Diagnosing behavior problems:** When people consistently behave in ways that conflict with their stated intentions, examine the setting for factors driving the behavior.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When manipulation is the intent:** Choice architecture can be used to nudge people toward good outcomes (healthy eating, retirement savings) or toward bad ones (dark patterns in UX). The ethics depend entirely on whose interests are served.
- **Over-attributing behavior to setting:** People do have stable character that persists across settings. Setting explains more than most people think, but not everything.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Define the desired behavior:** What action or decision do you want to occur more or less reliably?
2. **Map the current setting:** What physical, social, informational, and default-option features characterize the current environment?
3. **Identify setting factors driving unwanted behavior:** What environmental cues, defaults, or friction points are encouraging the wrong behavior?
4. **Design for the desired behavior:** Change defaults to the desired option; reduce friction for good choices; increase friction for bad ones; add cues that trigger the desired behavior.
5. **Implement in the real setting:** Unlike individual motivation, setting changes affect everyone in the environment automatically.
6. **Monitor behavior change:** Measure whether the setting change produced the expected behavior shift.
7. **Iterate:** Adjust the setting design based on what works, not what was planned.
