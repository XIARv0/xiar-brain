# Contrast

## Description

**Contrast** is the mental model describing how perception, evaluation, and attention are fundamentally relative — we notice and judge things not in isolation but against their immediate context and comparison. The same color looks different against a white background than a dark one. The same salary feels generous or insulting depending on what you were told to expect. The same argument is compelling or trivial depending on what alternatives were presented first.

- **Origins:** The contrast effect is one of the most robust findings in perceptual psychology, dating to early psychophysics. It was extended to decision-making by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's work on framing effects, and to influence by Robert Cialdini in *Influence* (1984), who documented how salesperson sequence (showing expensive items first) exploits contrast to inflate perceived value of subsequent items.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Sequential contrast:** Items presented in sequence are evaluated relative to what came before. A $500 suit after a $5,000 suit seems reasonable. A $500 suit after a $50 suit seems expensive.
    - **Simultaneous contrast:** Adjacent items affect each other's perceived value — a mediocre candidate looks stronger when surrounded by weaker ones, and weaker when surrounded by stronger ones.
    - **Anchoring as contrast:** The first number presented in a negotiation becomes the anchor against which all subsequent numbers are evaluated, regardless of its relevance.
    - **Contrast in communication:** Effective communicators use contrast deliberately — the powerful speech uses darkness to make the light feel brighter. The memorable idea is distinguished from the alternatives clearly.
    - **The manipulation risk:** Contrast is one of the most commonly exploited cognitive mechanisms in sales, negotiation, and propaganda — presenting a manipulative anchor to make the "real" offer seem reasonable by comparison.

- **Real-World Examples:** Car dealerships show expensive add-ons after the purchase decision — small amounts relative to the car price. Real estate agents show overpriced houses first to make the target listing seem reasonable. A salary negotiation where you name a high anchor first. A presentation that makes a data point feel significant by contrasting it to a dramatically different comparison.

---

## When to Use

- **Negotiation:** Use contrast strategically — anchor high to make your real position seem reasonable; recognize when you're being anchored against.
- **Communication design:** Use contrast to highlight key points — the important stands out against the mundane, the solution against the problem.
- **Decision framing:** When presenting options, consider what the contrast structure makes each look like.
- **Evaluating your own judgments:** Before concluding something is good or bad, ask: what am I comparing it to? Is that comparison appropriate?

---

## When to Avoid

- **When contrast misleads you:** Recognize when an inappropriate comparison is distorting your evaluation — especially in financial decisions where large amounts follow each other.
- **Manufacturing false contrast:** Creating artificial comparison points to manipulate others is ethically questionable and erodes trust when discovered.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify what is being judged:** What is the object of evaluation — a price, a person, an idea, a performance?
2. **Identify the contrast context:** What is it being compared to, implicitly or explicitly?
3. **Ask if the comparison is appropriate:** Is the reference point relevant to the actual value of the thing?
4. **Check for manipulation:** Is someone presenting a deliberately extreme comparison to make their offer seem better by contrast?
5. **Seek the right reference class:** Compare the item to genuinely similar alternatives, not artificially chosen ones.
6. **Use contrast intentionally in communication:** Structure your message so the important points stand out against the background.
7. **Reset anchors in negotiation:** When faced with an extreme anchor, explicitly reject it before engaging with any counter.
