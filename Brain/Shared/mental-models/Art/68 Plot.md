# Plot

## Description

**Plot** is the mental model of structuring events as a causal sequence — not just "what happened" but "why it happened, what it caused, and how it resolved." Plot imposes causal logic on events, transforming a list of facts into a meaningful story. Applied broadly, plot-thinking makes arguments more persuasive, decisions more legible, and strategies more communicable.

- **Origins:** Aristotle defined plot (mythos) in *Poetics* as "the arrangement of incidents" with a beginning, middle, and end — specifically a causal chain where one thing causes the next. In cognitive science, Roger Schank and Robert Abelson's script theory (1977) demonstrated that humans naturally understand experience through story-like causal schemas. Modern story structure frameworks (Robert McKee's *Story*, Joseph Campbell's Hero's Journey) operationalize plot as the universal architecture of human sense-making.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Causality, not chronology:** Plot is not a timeline — it is a chain of causes and effects. "The king died and then the queen died" is a timeline. "The king died and then the queen died of grief" is a plot.
    - **Conflict as the engine:** Plot requires an obstacle or conflict — a gap between the desired state and the current state. Without conflict, there is no plot, only description.
    - **Setup, conflict, resolution:** The three-act structure is universal because it maps onto how humans process problems: establish the world and stakes, introduce the complication, resolve it.
    - **Implication for persuasion:** Arguments structured as plots are far more persuasive than arguments structured as lists of facts. A narrative with a problem (conflict), analysis (rising action), and recommendation (resolution) is remembered and acted on.
    - **Implication for strategy:** A strategy expressed as a plot — the current situation (setup), the challenge or opportunity (conflict), the path forward (resolution) — is infinitely more motivating than a strategy expressed as a goal and a list of actions.

- **Real-World Examples:** The most effective business case studies are plots: a company faced a competitive threat (conflict), tried various solutions (rising action), and found the one that worked (resolution). Lincoln's Gettysburg Address is a three-act plot compressed into 272 words. The iPhone launch presentation was a carefully constructed plot: the problem of three unsatisfying devices (conflict), the solution (resolution), the stakes (setup).

---

## When to Use

- **Any persuasive communication:** Structure your argument as a plot — problem/opportunity, analysis, recommendation — rather than a data dump.
- **Strategy and vision documents:** Express strategy as a coherent narrative with a conflict to resolve and a path to resolution.
- **Teaching and explanation:** Use plot structure to teach complex concepts — the "before state," the "complication," and the "after state" make abstract ideas concrete and memorable.
- **Diagnosis:** When analyzing failures, plot-structure your analysis — what was the setup, where did conflict arise, what caused the bad outcome?

---

## When to Avoid

- **Technical documentation:** Specifications, reference material, and procedures need precision and completeness, not plot structure.
- **When the conflict is unresolved:** Don't force a resolution onto a situation that genuinely has none — premature closure is misleading.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the setup:** What is the current state of affairs? What are the stakes? What does the audience need to know as context?
2. **Identify the conflict:** What problem, tension, or gap exists? What is preventing the desired outcome?
3. **Develop the rising action:** What analysis, attempts, or factors are relevant to resolving the conflict?
4. **Identify the resolution:** What is the insight, decision, or recommendation that resolves the conflict?
5. **Check causality:** Is each step linked to the next by cause and effect, or are you listing events chronologically?
6. **Cut what doesn't serve the plot:** Anything that doesn't advance the conflict toward resolution is noise — remove it.
7. **Lead with the conflict:** In business communication, state the problem or opportunity first — don't make the audience wait through setup to find out why they should care.
