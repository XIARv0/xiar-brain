# Character

## Description

**Character** is the mental model describing the consistent pattern of values, responses, and choices that define a person or organization under pressure. Unlike personality (surface traits) or reputation (what others believe), character is revealed through behavior when the stakes are high and the easy choice differs from the right one. Applied broadly, it is the most reliable predictor of future behavior.

- **Origins:** Aristotle distinguished character (ethos) as one of the three modes of persuasion — and as the most powerful, because an audience trusts a person of good character more than they trust arguments. In narrative theory, character is revealed through action under pressure, not description. In psychology, character strength research (Martin Seligman's positive psychology) and personality psychology (the Big Five) both grapple with the stable traits that predict behavior across contexts.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Character is revealed under stress:** Comfortable conditions don't reveal character because the easy and right choices coincide. High-pressure situations, ethical dilemmas, and costly commitments are where character shows.
    - **Character is consistent:** By definition, a person's character should be visible across many different situations. Inconsistency signals either that character is still developing, or that the "character" in easier situations was performance, not substance.
    - **Character vs. capability:** In hiring, partnerships, and relationships, character failures are far more damaging than capability gaps. Skills can be learned; character is deeply stable.
    - **Organizational character:** Organizations have character — the stable pattern of decisions made when convenience conflicts with values. It is revealed by how they treat customers, employees, and partners when no one is watching.
    - **Character assessment:** The best evidence of character is past behavior in relevant high-stakes situations — not stated values, not performance under observation.

- **Real-World Examples:** Warren Buffett: "When you're looking for somebody to hire, you look for three qualities: integrity, intelligence, and energy. But the most important is integrity, because if they don't have that, the other two qualities will kill you." A CEO who takes a salary cut during layoffs instead of laying off lower-paid workers reveals character. A company that silently changes a product to reduce quality rather than raise prices reveals it differently.

---

## When to Use

- **Hiring and partnership decisions:** Weight character evidence (track record under pressure) heavily relative to stated values and performance under observation.
- **Predicting behavior:** In ongoing relationships, understand that character is the best predictor of future behavior, especially in novel high-stakes situations.
- **Assessing organizations:** Look for the pattern of decisions made when convenience and values conflict to understand a company's real character.
- **Self-development:** Use the character framework to identify the gap between your stated values and your actual behavior under pressure.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Conflating one bad decision with character failure:** Everyone makes mistakes. Character is a pattern, not a single incident.
- **Ignoring situational factors:** Character and situation interact — an otherwise honest person may behave poorly under extreme duress. Consider the context of past behavior.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Define what character dimensions matter:** For this context, what values and behaviors are most important — integrity, courage, generosity, discipline?
2. **Seek behavioral evidence:** Ask for specific examples of high-stakes, high-pressure situations. What did they actually do?
3. **Look for consistency:** Is the pattern of behavior consistent across different contexts and over time?
4. **Distinguish performance from character:** Is the person behaving this way because they're being observed, or because it's who they are?
5. **Identify stress-test situations:** Has this person or organization faced a genuine conflict between self-interest and their stated values? What happened?
6. **Weight character evidence heavily in consequential decisions:** In partnerships, hiring, and long-term relationships, character failure is rarely recoverable.
7. **Monitor for character drift:** Character can change under sustained pressure or environmental change — track it over time, not just at one point.
