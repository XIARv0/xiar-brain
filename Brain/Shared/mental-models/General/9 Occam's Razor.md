# Occam's Razor

## Description

**Occam’s Razor** (also known as the Law of Parsimony) is a principle of logic and problem-solving that states: when presented with competing hypotheses that make the same prediction, one should select the solution with the fewest assumptions. It is not a law of nature that guarantees truth, but a heuristic tool to prioritize the most likely explanation.

- **Origins:** Named after the 14th-century English logician and Franciscan friar **William of Ockham**, who famously argued _pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate_ (plurality should not be posited without necessity). While the specific phrasing evolved over centuries, the core concept has become a bedrock of the scientific method.
    
- **Key Principles:**
    
    - **Parsimony:** The idea that "nature operates in the shortest way possible." Complexity should be treated as a "cost" that must be justified by superior explanatory power.
        
    - **Assumption Minimization:** Every assumption added to a hypothesis increases the probability that the hypothesis is false. By cutting away unnecessary assumptions (the "razor"), we arrive at a more robust conclusion.
        
- **Real-World Example:** In medicine, students are taught, "When you hear hoofbeats, think of horses, not zebras." If a patient exhibits common cold symptoms, the most likely (simplest) explanation is a viral infection, not a rare tropical disease. While the zebra is _possible_, it requires too many unlikely assumptions.
    

---

## When to Use

- **Scientific Inquiry:** When choosing between two theories that explain the same data, the theory requiring fewer auxiliary hypotheses is preferred.
    
- **Debugging Complex Systems:** When code, machinery, or business processes fail, Occam’s Razor suggests looking for the simplest point of failure (e.g., a disconnected wire) before assuming a systemic, catastrophic error.
    
- **Effective Communication:** In business or technical writing, if a point can be explained simply without jargon, simplicity usually conveys intent more effectively and reduces the risk of misunderstanding.
    
- **Data Modeling:** To avoid "overfitting," where a model becomes so complex that it explains the noise in the data rather than the underlying trend.
    

---

## When to Avoid

- **Complexity-Heavy Domains:** In biology, geopolitics, or human behavior, the "simple" answer is often dangerously wrong because the systems themselves are fundamentally non-linear and multi-causal.
    
- **When Data is Sparse:** If the simplest explanation doesn't actually fit the facts, Occam’s Razor should not be used to ignore data points that don't conform to your simple narrative.
    
- **To Dismiss Reality:** It should not be used as an excuse to avoid investigating inconvenient, complex truths. Just because an explanation is complex does not make it false.
    

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Define the Problem:** Clearly articulate the specific phenomenon you are trying to explain.
    
2. **Generate Hypotheses:** List all possible explanations that account for the observed evidence.
    
3. **Audit Assumptions:** For each hypothesis, list the "hidden" assumptions required to make it true. (e.g., "For this to be true, I have to assume X, Y, and Z also happened.")
    
4. **Compare Parsimony:** Eliminate any hypothesis that relies on an excessive number of unverified assumptions compared to others.
    
5. **Test the Simplest Hypothesis:** Before committing to a complex theory, try to falsify the simplest one. Is there any evidence that definitively contradicts it?
    
6. **Iterate:** If the simplest hypothesis is contradicted by evidence, move to the next "simplest" explanation on your list and repeat.
    
7. **Document the Decision:** Explicitly note which assumptions you are accepting as "ground truth" to allow for future revision if new data emerges.
