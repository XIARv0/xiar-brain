# Circle of Competence

## Description

The **Circle of Competence** is a mental model popularized by Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger to describe the boundary between a person’s actual knowledge and the areas where they lack expertise. The core premise is simple: you don't need to be an expert in everything, but you must be exceptionally clear about where your expertise ends.

The concept first gained prominence in Buffett’s 1996 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, where he noted that for an investor, "What is needed is the ability to correctly evaluate selected businesses. Note that word 'selected.' You don't have to be an expert on every company... You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence."

**Key Principles:**

- **The Perimeter is Paramount:** The size of the circle is less important than knowing where the edge lies. Operating even slightly outside your perimeter significantly increases the risk of catastrophic failure.
    
- **Intellectual Honesty:** It requires the ego-stripping discipline to say, "I don't know."
    
- **Building Through Experience:** A true circle of competence is built over years of skin-in-the-game, feedback loops, and deep study, not just casual reading.
    

**Real-World Example:** In the late 1990s during the Dot-com bubble, Warren Buffett famously avoided investing in tech stocks despite immense market pressure. He admitted he did not understand their long-term economics. While he was criticized for being "out of touch," his adherence to his circle protected Berkshire Hathaway when the bubble eventually burst.

---

## When to Use

- **Capital Allocation:** When deciding where to invest money, time, or energy. If you cannot explain the "why" of an opportunity to a five-year-old, it is likely outside your circle.
    
- **Career Strategy:** When choosing a niche or industry to specialize in. High-leverage success usually comes from being in the top 5% of a specific field rather than the top 50% of many.
    
- **Risk Management:** When facing a high-stakes decision where the "downside" is significant. Identifying that a problem is outside your circle is the first step toward seeking external expertise.
    
- **Delegation:** When building a team, use this model to identify your own gaps so you can hire people whose circles overlap with your voids.
    

---

## When to Avoid

- **The "Luddite" Trap:** Do not use the circle of competence as an excuse for intellectual laziness or a refusal to adapt to inevitable industry shifts (e.g., a taxi driver ignoring the rise of ride-sharing).
    
- **Innovation Phases:** In the early stages of a startup or a creative project, you often _must_ operate outside your circle to discover new territory. Rigidity here can stifle growth.
    
- **Interdisciplinary Problems:** Some problems require "latticework" thinking (as Munger suggests). If you stay strictly within one circle, you may fall victim to the "Man with a Hammer" syndrome, where every problem looks like a nail.
    

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Define Your Core:** List the topics, industries, or skills where you have a proven track record of success and deep, firsthand experience.
    
2. **Audit the "Perimeter":** Look at your recent failures. Did they occur because you ventured into an area where you had only surface-level knowledge? Mark these as "Outside."
    
3. **The "Why" Test:** For any given opportunity, ask: _Do I understand the fundamental drivers of this, or am I relying on someone else's "expert" opinion?_ If it's the latter, you are outside your circle.
    
4. **Identify the "Too Hard" Pile:** Borrowing from Munger, create a mental (or physical) "Too Hard" pile. If a problem is too complex or outside your expertise, move it to this pile immediately rather than guessing.
    
5. **Build or Borrow:** If a task is outside your circle but necessary:
    
    - **Build:** Commit to a long-term learning path to expand your circle.
        
    - **Borrow:** Find a partner or specialist whose circle covers your gap.
        
6. **Maintain the Circle:** Constantly update your knowledge. Competence decays if not maintained through active practice and feedback.
