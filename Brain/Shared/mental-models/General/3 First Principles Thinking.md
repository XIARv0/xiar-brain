# First Principles Thinking

## Description

**First Principles Thinking** is a mode of inquiry that requires breaking a process or problem down to its fundamental, foundational truths—elements that cannot be deduced from any other—and building a solution from the ground up. Instead of "reasoning by analogy" (looking at how others have solved a problem and iterating on their methods), this model encourages a "physics-style" approach to logic.

- **Origins:** The concept dates back to **Aristotle**, who defined a first principle as "the first basis from which a thing is known." In modern times, it has been heavily popularized by **Elon Musk**, who uses it to challenge industry norms in aerospace and automotive engineering.
    
- **Key Principles:** It identifies the difference between **functional requirements** (what must be achieved) and **inherited assumptions** (how it has always been done). By discarding the latter, one can find non-linear paths to innovation.
    
- **Real-World Example:** When founding SpaceX, Musk was told that rockets were prohibitively expensive. Instead of buying a finished rocket, he applied first principles: he calculated the raw material costs (aluminum, titanium, copper, carbon fiber) on the commodities market. He discovered that the materials cost only about **2%** of a typical rocket's price. By manufacturing in-house and rethinking the assembly, SpaceX drastically reduced the cost of reaching orbit.
    

---

## When to Use

- **True Innovation:** When you want to create something entirely new rather than an incremental improvement on an existing product.
    
- **Breaking Stagnation:** When a project or industry has hit a "plateau" and traditional methods are no longer yielding results.
    
- **Cost Reduction:** When "industry standards" for pricing seem arbitrary or inflated. Breaking a product down into its constituent parts often reveals hidden efficiencies.
    
- **Complex Problem Solving:** When a problem has many moving parts and "best practices" seem to conflict or fail.
    

---

## When to Avoid

- **Time-Sensitive Situations:** Deconstructing a problem to its atoms is mentally taxing and time-consuming. If a fire needs to be put out immediately, use a proven heuristic or analogy.
    
- **Social and Cultural Norms:** Human relationships often rely on shared conventions and "analogy." Applying cold, reductive logic to emotional or cultural traditions can lead to social friction or misunderstanding.
    
- **Proven, Low-Stakes Processes:** If you are performing a routine task where the existing method is 95% efficient, the energy required for first-principles deconstruction likely outweighs the 5% gain.
    

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify and Define the Objective:** Clearly state what you are trying to achieve. Strip away the "how" and focus entirely on the "what."
    
2. **Identify Current Assumptions:** List the common "rules," "best practices," and "limitations" associated with the problem. Ask: "Why do we do it this way?"
    
3. **Deconstruct into Fundamental Truths:** Break the problem down until you reach "axioms"—facts that are undeniably true and cannot be broken down further. (e.g., The laws of physics, the price of raw materials, the basic biological needs of a user).
    
4. **Analyze the Deconstructed Parts:** Evaluate each fundamental truth independently. Are the traditional barriers actually physical laws, or just social/economic habits?
    
5. **Create a New Solution from Scratch:** Use the fundamental truths to build a new path toward your objective. Avoid looking at existing solutions during this phase to prevent "anchoring" your thoughts to the old way.
    
6. **Test and Iterate:** Compare your new "first principles" solution against the objective. Refine based on practical feedback while ensuring you don't slip back into reasoning by analogy.
