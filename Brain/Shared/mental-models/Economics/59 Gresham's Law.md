# Gresham's Law

## Description

**Gresham's Law** states that "bad money drives out good" — when two forms of currency coexist with the same face value but different intrinsic worth, people spend the debased currency and hoard the valuable one. Applied as a general mental model, it describes the broader phenomenon where low-quality elements in any mixed system systematically crowd out high-quality ones when they are treated as equivalent.

- **Origins:** Named for Sir Thomas Gresham, financial adviser to Queen Elizabeth I, who observed the phenomenon in Tudor England when the government debased coins (mixing in base metals) while issuing them at the same face value as pure-metal coins. The pure coins quickly disappeared from circulation — hoarded or melted. The formal statement was made by economist Henry Dunning Macleod in 1858. Charlie Munger has repeatedly cited this model as one of the most broadly applicable in social science.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **The equivalence requirement:** Gresham's Law applies when different-quality items are treated as interchangeable. When bad and good are indistinguishable to market participants (or forced to be equivalent by regulation), bad drives out good.
    - **Adverse selection as generalized Gresham:** In any market with information asymmetry, if buyers cannot distinguish quality, high-quality goods are withdrawn and low-quality goods flood in (Akerlof's "Market for Lemons").
    - **Organizational Gresham:** Bad employees who survive selection drive out good ones — through frustration, lowered standards, and cultural erosion. One toxic high-performer can degrade an entire team.
    - **Information Gresham:** In media environments where false and true information circulate freely, misinformation spreads faster (it is cheaper to produce and more engaging) and crowds out accurate information.
    - **The fix is differentiation:** Gresham's Law is defeated when quality can be distinguished and rewarded — pure coins command a premium, certified professionals command higher fees, trusted media brands command subscription revenue.

- **Real-World Examples:** The market for used cars (Akerlof's lemons) — sellers know the car's quality but buyers don't, so high-quality cars are withdrawn because their owners can't get a fair price. Social media platforms where engagement-optimized misinformation drives out slower, more nuanced accurate reporting. A company where mediocre employees who are politically skilled get promoted, driving out high performers who lack political savvy.

---

## When to Use

- **Diagnosing quality degradation:** When a system's average quality is declining, look for the Gresham mechanism — are low-quality inputs being treated as equivalent to high-quality ones?
- **Information evaluation:** In any information market, assess whether the incentives favor production of accurate vs. engaging content.
- **Hiring and culture:** Identify whether your selection and retention systems are accidentally selecting for bad culture while good employees leave.
- **Market design:** When designing any market or platform, build quality signals and differentiation mechanisms that prevent Gresham dynamics.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When quality is easily observable:** Gresham's Law requires equivalence or indistinguishability. When quality differences are clear and priced, the mechanism doesn't apply.
- **Assuming the "bad" is always malicious:** Gresham dynamics can emerge from good-faith actors who simply respond to incentives — not from deliberate debasement.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the mixed-quality system:** Where are high and low quality items treated as equivalent or indistinguishable?
2. **Identify the equivalence mechanism:** What law, policy, perception, or market structure forces the equivalence?
3. **Trace the crowding-out dynamic:** How does the presence of low-quality items make it harder for high-quality ones to survive?
4. **Find the information asymmetry:** Who knows the quality and who doesn't? This gap enables Gresham dynamics.
5. **Design quality signals:** How can high-quality items be credibly distinguished? (Certification, reputation, transparency, warranties.)
6. **Remove forced equivalence:** If a policy or norm forces the equivalence, change it to allow quality to be priced and rewarded.
7. **Monitor leading indicators:** Decreasing participation by high-quality actors (employees, sellers, contributors) is an early warning that Gresham dynamics are operating.
