# Bubbles

## Description

**Bubbles** are episodes in which asset prices rise far above fundamental value, sustained by the self-reinforcing belief that prices will continue rising — attracting more buyers, which drives prices further, until the belief collapses and prices crash. Bubbles occur not because participants are irrational, but because rational individual responses to incentives produce collectively irrational outcomes.

- **Origins:** The Dutch Tulip Mania (1637), the South Sea Bubble (1720), and countless subsequent episodes established the pattern. Economist Hyman Minsky formalized bubble dynamics in the "Minsky Moment" — the point where debt-fueled speculation collapses. Robert Shiller won the Nobel Prize partly for his work identifying bubble conditions through cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratios. Charles Kindleberger's *Manias, Panics, and Crashes* (1978) remains the definitive historical account.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **The five stages (Minsky/Kindleberger model):** Displacement (new technology or opportunity) → Boom (credit expansion, rising prices) → Euphoria (everyone is getting rich, skeptics are mocked) → Profit-taking (smart money exits) → Panic (price collapse, credit contraction).
    - **Rational individual, irrational collective:** Each participant may be rationally riding the bubble — knowing it will end, but betting they'll exit in time. Collectively, this sustains the bubble and guarantees the crash.
    - **Reflexivity (Soros):** Rising prices attract buyers who drive prices further — the feedback between beliefs and prices is self-reinforcing until it reverses.
    - **Narrative bubbles:** Bubbles require a compelling story that justifies the prices — "this time is different," "new paradigm," "infinite growth." Narratives make price levels feel rational.
    - **Credit as accelerant:** Bubbles are typically fueled by credit expansion — leverage amplifies both the rise and the collapse.
    - **Identification difficulty:** Identifying a bubble in real time requires a contrarian assessment of value when consensus is strongly positive. This is psychologically and professionally costly.

- **Real-World Examples:** The dot-com bubble (1995-2000) — companies with no revenue trading at hundreds of times imaginary future earnings. The U.S. housing bubble (2003-2008) — prices rising far above income fundamentals, sustained by loose credit and the belief that housing prices never fall nationally. The crypto bubble of 2021 — assets with no cash flows trading at multi-trillion-dollar valuations.

---

## When to Use

- **Asset valuation:** Compare current prices to long-run fundamental metrics (P/E ratios, price-to-book, yield comparisons to alternatives) to identify departures from historical norms.
- **Investment positioning:** During bubble conditions, the correct response is not necessarily to short (the bubble can continue far longer than you can remain solvent) but to reduce exposure and avoid leverage.
- **Business planning:** Distinguish between genuine industry growth and bubble-driven demand — business models built on bubble demand collapse when the bubble does.
- **Recognizing euphoria signals:** When contrarians are being mocked, when "this time is different" is mainstream, when leverage is widespread — these are bubble indicators.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Calling everything a bubble:** Many assets appreciate for fundamental reasons. "Bubble" is overused — apply it specifically when prices are demonstrably disconnected from plausible fundamental value.
- **Shorting too early:** Bubbles can last far longer than seems possible. Knowing something is a bubble and successfully timing the exit are very different skills.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Assess fundamental value:** What is the asset worth based on cash flows, comparable assets, or replacement cost?
2. **Compare to current price:** How large is the gap between current price and fundamental value?
3. **Identify the narrative:** What story justifies the price? How plausible is it on first principles?
4. **Look for bubble indicators:** Widespread leverage, retail investor euphoria, mockery of skeptics, "this time is different" reasoning.
5. **Identify which stage you're in:** Displacement, boom, euphoria, profit-taking, or panic?
6. **Assess exit liquidity:** If this is a bubble, how easily can you exit when sentiment turns?
7. **Decide on positioning:** Reduce leverage, reduce exposure, or exit — but don't assume you can time the top.
8. **Protect against contagion:** If the bubble is in a related market, assess how a crash there affects your non-bubble positions through credit contraction and panic spillover.
