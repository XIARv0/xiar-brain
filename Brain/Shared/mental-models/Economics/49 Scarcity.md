# Scarcity

## Description

**Scarcity** is the foundational problem of economics: human wants are unlimited, but the resources to satisfy them — time, money, attention, materials, talent — are finite. Every decision is therefore a choice about allocation, and every choice involves giving up something else. Scarcity is not a temporary condition to be solved; it is the permanent structure of existence that makes decision-making necessary.

- **Origins:** Scarcity as the core of economic reasoning was formalized by Lionel Robbins in 1932: "Economics is the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses." Behavioral economists Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir extended the concept in *Scarcity: Why Having Too Little Means So Much* (2013), showing that perceived scarcity — of money, time, or social connection — creates a "scarcity mindset" that tunnels cognition and impairs decision-making.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Opportunity cost:** Every use of a scarce resource forecloses other uses. The true cost of any choice is the best alternative foregone — not just the financial cost.
    - **Scarcity drives value:** In markets, prices reflect scarcity relative to demand. What is abundant is cheap; what is scarce and desired is expensive.
    - **Artificial scarcity:** Scarcity can be created (patents, branding, limited editions) to capture value in otherwise abundant markets.
    - **The scarcity mindset:** Mullainathan and Shafir found that people experiencing scarcity (of money or time) focus intensely on the scarce resource but make worse decisions overall — tunneling out everything else. Poverty is partly a cognitive tax.
    - **Attention scarcity:** In the information age, the scarcest resource is not information but human attention. The attention economy treats human attention as the resource to be allocated.

- **Real-World Examples:** A startup has a scarcity of cash, time, and talent — every decision to use these is a trade-off. A surgeon's attention is scarce — scheduling complex operations first in the morning (when cognitive resources are highest) is an allocation of scarce mental resource. Luxury brands manufacture scarcity (limited editions, exclusivity) to maintain high prices for otherwise replicable goods.

---

## When to Use

- **Resource allocation decisions:** Whenever allocating time, money, attention, or talent — make the scarcity and opportunity costs explicit.
- **Pricing and value strategy:** Understand the scarcity of your product or service from the buyer's perspective.
- **Diagnosing poor decisions:** When someone is making consistently bad choices, ask whether a scarcity mindset (stress, poverty, time pressure) is tunneling their cognition.
- **Evaluating business models:** Identify the scarce resource your business depends on and whether it is durable.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Manufactured scarcity in trust-based relationships:** Artificially creating urgency or exclusivity backfires when discovered — it's a short-term tactic with long-term costs.
- **When abundance is the actual condition:** Not every resource is genuinely scarce. Digital goods (software, information) have near-zero marginal cost — treating them with scarcity logic misses the economics.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the scarce resource:** What is genuinely limited in this situation — time, money, attention, talent, materials?
2. **List the alternative uses:** What are all the ways this scarce resource could be used?
3. **Identify the opportunity cost:** What is the best alternative foregone by making this choice?
4. **Check for scarcity mindset effects:** Is a perceived scarcity causing tunneling — focusing too narrowly and missing important information?
5. **Evaluate whether scarcity is real or artificial:** Is the limitation genuinely structural, or is it a policy or belief that could change?
6. **Prioritize by value per unit of scarce resource:** Rank options by the value they create per unit of the scarce input.
7. **Protect the scarcest resource:** Often the most important decision is guarding the scarcest resource (often time or attention) from low-value uses.
