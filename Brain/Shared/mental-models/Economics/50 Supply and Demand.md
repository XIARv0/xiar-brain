# Supply and Demand

## Description

**Supply and Demand** is the fundamental mechanism by which markets coordinate the decisions of buyers and sellers through prices. When demand exceeds supply, prices rise — attracting more supply and reducing demand until balance is restored. When supply exceeds demand, prices fall — reducing supply and increasing demand. This self-correcting mechanism is one of the most powerful and elegant models in social science.

- **Origins:** Formalized by Alfred Marshall in *Principles of Economics* (1890), though its logic was recognized earlier by Adam Smith. The model describes how decentralized price signals coordinate billions of individual decisions without any central planner — what Hayek called the "price system" as an information aggregation mechanism.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Price as information:** Prices encode dispersed information about scarcity and value — a rising price signals that something is becoming scarcer or more desired, coordinating suppliers to produce more and consumers to economize.
    - **Equilibrium price:** The market-clearing price where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded.
    - **Shifts vs. movements:** A change in price causes movement along the supply or demand curve. A change in income, preferences, or input costs shifts the entire curve.
    - **Elasticity:** How sensitive supply or demand is to price changes. Inelastic demand (insulin, gasoline) means price changes don't reduce consumption much. Elastic demand means small price changes produce large quantity changes.
    - **Price controls distort:** Artificially setting prices above or below equilibrium creates surpluses (price floors) or shortages (price ceilings) by breaking the coordination mechanism.
    - **Applies beyond markets:** Supply and demand logic governs labor markets (wages), attention markets (content), dating markets (relationship formation), and talent markets (salary negotiation).

- **Real-World Examples:** The 2021 semiconductor shortage — demand surged (remote work, EVs) while supply was constrained — causing price spikes and rationing. Rent control (price ceiling below equilibrium) creates housing shortages by reducing incentive to supply. The market for software engineers — high demand, constrained supply — drives exceptional wages.

---

## When to Use

- **Pricing decisions:** Use supply/demand analysis to predict whether price increases will be absorbed or cause demand destruction.
- **Market entry analysis:** Assess whether current prices signal a gap between demand and supply that a new entrant can fill.
- **Labor and talent strategy:** Understand that hiring difficulty reflects a supply/demand imbalance in the talent market — which suggests either raising price (salary) or reducing demand (automation, outsourcing).
- **Predicting policy effects:** Before implementing a price control, subsidy, or tax, use supply/demand analysis to trace the likely distortions.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Markets with network effects or winner-take-most dynamics:** Standard supply/demand models assume competitive markets. Platforms and network-effect businesses don't equilibrate the same way.
- **Short-run analysis of sticky prices:** In the short run, many prices don't adjust quickly (wages, rents, long-term contracts) — standard equilibrium analysis overstates speed of adjustment.
- **Non-market goods:** Values like justice, love, and dignity resist supply/demand framing in ways that matter ethically even if the model technically applies.

## Thinking Steps

1. **Define the market:** What good or service? What geography and time horizon?
2. **Map the demand curve:** Who are the buyers? How sensitive is their quantity demanded to price?
3. **Map the supply curve:** Who are the suppliers? What drives their cost and production decisions?
4. **Find the equilibrium:** Where do supply and demand balance? What price clears the market?
5. **Identify the elasticities:** How responsive are buyers and sellers to price changes?
6. **Anticipate shifts:** What events could shift supply or demand (technological change, income change, new entrants, regulations)?
7. **Evaluate any distortions:** Are there price controls, subsidies, or taxes? Trace their effects on equilibrium quantity and who gains/loses.
8. **Apply to your decision:** Based on the analysis, what does the supply/demand structure imply for your strategy?
