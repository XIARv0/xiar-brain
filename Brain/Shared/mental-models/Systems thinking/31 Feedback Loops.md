# Feedback Loops

## Description

**Feedback Loops** are one of the most powerful structures in any complex system — they describe how the output of a process feeds back as input, either amplifying or correcting the process. Understanding which type of loop is operating in a system allows you to predict its behavior, find leverage points, and avoid being blindsided by runaway or stagnating dynamics.

- **Origins:** Rooted in cybernetics (Norbert Wiener, 1948) and formalized for general systems by Donella Meadows in *Thinking in Systems* (2008). Meadows argued that identifying feedback loops is the single most important step in understanding any system.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Reinforcing loops (positive feedback):** The output amplifies the cause. More of A produces more of B, which produces more of A. Examples: compound interest, viral spread, learning curves, network effects. These create exponential growth — or collapse.
    - **Balancing loops (negative feedback):** The output opposes the cause, pushing the system toward a target or equilibrium. Examples: a thermostat, hunger/eating, market price corrections. These create stability and self-correction.
    - **Delays:** Time lags between cause and effect are the source of most system oscillation and overshoot. Acting before feedback arrives causes overcorrection.
    - **Dominance shifts:** Systems often contain both loop types; which one dominates at a given moment determines the system's behavior.

- **Real-World Examples:** A startup with strong word-of-mouth is in a reinforcing loop — more users generate more reviews, which generate more users. A central bank raising rates to control inflation is a balancing loop. The 2008 financial crisis was partly a reinforcing loop of rising prices → more lending → rising prices until the loop broke.

---

## When to Use

- **Diagnosing growth or stagnation:** Identify whether reinforcing loops are driving growth and whether balancing loops are dampening it.
- **Predicting system behavior:** Before intervening in a system, map the loops to anticipate whether your action will be amplified or corrected.
- **Finding leverage points:** Donella Meadows identified feedback loop strength and direction as high-leverage intervention points.
- **Managing delays:** When there's a lag between action and outcome, recognizing the balancing loop prevents the overcorrection that causes oscillation.
- **Designing incentive systems:** Ensure that desired behaviors are reinforced and undesired behaviors are corrected by the system's own loops.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Oversimplifying complex systems:** Real systems have dozens of interlocking loops; mapping only the obvious ones can give false confidence in predictions.
- **Ignoring thresholds:** Some systems behave linearly until a threshold is crossed, at which point loop dynamics shift — don't extrapolate past these tipping points.
- **Forcing the model onto linear problems:** Not everything is a loop; some processes are genuinely one-directional and don't benefit from feedback framing.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Define the system boundary:** What is included in this system? What flows in and out?
2. **Identify key variables:** What are the measurable stocks and flows that matter most?
3. **Trace the connections:** For each variable, ask: what does it affect, and what affects it?
4. **Classify each loop:** Is it reinforcing (same-direction effects) or balancing (opposite-direction effects)?
5. **Identify delays:** Where are the significant time lags between cause and effect?
6. **Determine which loop dominates now:** Is the system currently in growth, correction, oscillation, or collapse?
7. **Find the leverage point:** Which loop, if strengthened or weakened, would most change the system's behavior?
8. **Act and observe:** Intervene, then watch for the feedback — does the system respond as predicted?
