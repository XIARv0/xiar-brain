# Algorithms

## Description

**Algorithms** as a mental model is the practice of making complex, repeatable decisions through explicit, pre-specified rules rather than in-the-moment judgment — removing variability, bias, and fatigue from processes that can be standardized. An algorithm is a finite, ordered set of instructions that, given a defined input, produces a consistent output.

- **Origins:** The term derives from Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, a 9th-century Persian mathematician. In modern decision science, the model was powerfully advanced by Atul Gawande in *The Checklist Manifesto* (2009), which demonstrated that checklists (simple algorithms) dramatically reduced errors in aviation and surgery. Daniel Kahneman's research showed that simple actuarial algorithms consistently outperform expert clinical judgment in prediction tasks.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Consistency over cleverness:** Algorithms remove the variance introduced by mood, fatigue, recency bias, and overconfidence. Consistent execution of a good strategy beats inconsistent execution of a great one.
    - **Checklists as the simplest algorithm:** A checklist is an algorithm for humans — it ensures steps are not skipped under pressure or distraction.
    - **Rules vs. judgment:** Algorithms are appropriate when the decision is repeatable and the key variables are known. Novel, high-context situations require judgment.
    - **Kahneman's finding:** In domains with clear outcome data (loan defaults, recidivism, medical diagnosis), simple statistical models outperform expert intuition because they don't get distracted by irrelevant information.
    - **Compounding through consistency:** An algorithm applied consistently compounds over thousands of decisions; judgment applied inconsistently does not.

- **Real-World Examples:** Warren Buffett's investment criteria function as an algorithm — defined filters that prevent emotional decisions. Pre-flight checklists that have saved countless lives. A/B testing frameworks that algorithmically compare options against objective criteria. Hiring rubrics that ensure all candidates are evaluated on the same dimensions.

---

## When to Use

- **Repeatable, high-volume decisions:** When you make the same type of decision frequently, codify the criteria into a rule to reduce cognitive load and ensure consistency.
- **High-stakes processes where errors are catastrophic:** Checklists prevent critical steps from being skipped under time pressure or stress.
- **When bias is a concern:** Algorithmic evaluation reduces favoritism and inconsistency in hiring, lending, and performance reviews.
- **Pre-commitment strategies:** Define decision rules in advance (when to sell a stock, when to fire a vendor) to prevent in-the-moment rationalization.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Novel, unprecedented situations:** Algorithms trained on past data fail in genuinely new contexts where the rules no longer apply.
- **High-context human interactions:** Relationships, negotiations, and creative work require reading subtle cues that no algorithm captures.
- **When the algorithm itself is the problem:** Rigid adherence to flawed rules is worse than flexible judgment. Algorithms must be periodically reviewed and updated.
- **When false precision misleads:** A numerical score from a checklist can create unwarranted confidence in a fundamentally uncertain judgment.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the decision type:** Is this decision repeatable, with defined inputs and measurable outcomes?
2. **List the relevant criteria:** What factors actually predict good outcomes in this domain? (Use data, not intuition.)
3. **Assign weights or thresholds:** Which criteria are must-haves vs. nice-to-haves?
4. **Write the explicit rules:** "If X, then Y" — remove ambiguity from the decision process.
5. **Test against historical cases:** Apply the algorithm to past decisions. Does it produce good outcomes?
6. **Implement and commit:** Use the algorithm for real decisions without overriding it except in documented exceptional cases.
7. **Track outcomes:** Build a feedback loop to evaluate whether the algorithm is working.
8. **Update periodically:** As the environment changes, revise the algorithm's inputs and rules.
