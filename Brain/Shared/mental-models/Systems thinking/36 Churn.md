# Churn

## Description

**Churn** is the rate at which a system loses existing elements — customers, employees, members, energy — and must replace them just to maintain its current level. It is the "leaky bucket" problem: no matter how much you pour in at the top, if the hole at the bottom is large enough, the bucket never fills. Churn is one of the most underappreciated constraints on growth and sustainability.

- **Origins:** The term originates in subscription businesses and telecommunications, where "churn rate" measures the percentage of subscribers who cancel in a given period. It was formalized as a key business metric in the SaaS era, where David Skok and others demonstrated that high churn makes growth mathematically impossible regardless of new customer acquisition. The concept applies broadly beyond business to any stock-and-flow system.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **The leaky bucket:** If churn rate exceeds the acquisition rate, the system shrinks. If they are equal, the system is treading water despite active effort.
    - **Churn compounds negatively:** High churn means you are constantly selling to replace lost value rather than building on it — the compounding effect works in reverse.
    - **The break-even acquisition rate:** To grow, you must acquire at a rate greater than churn. As the installed base grows, the absolute number of churned units grows even at a constant churn rate — requiring ever-increasing acquisition to maintain growth.
    - **Lifetime value depends on churn:** Customer lifetime value (LTV) = Average Revenue Per User ÷ Churn Rate. A 2% monthly churn means average customer life of 50 months; 10% churn means 10 months. This transforms unit economics entirely.
    - **Churn has causes:** Unlike random loss, most churn has identifiable causes — dissatisfaction, better alternatives, changing needs, poor onboarding. Fix the cause, not just the number.

- **Real-World Examples:** A SaaS company with 5% monthly churn replaces its entire customer base every 20 months — making long-term compounding impossible. A company with 20% annual employee turnover spends enormous resources recruiting and training just to stay static. A community that loses members faster than it adds them gradually hollows out despite seeming activity.

---

## When to Use

- **Evaluating business sustainability:** Before focusing on growth, audit whether the existing base is stable.
- **Diagnosing growth stagnation:** When growth efforts aren't producing results, churn may be canceling gains.
- **Comparing business quality:** Lower churn businesses have structurally better economics — use it as a quality indicator when evaluating businesses or investments.
- **People and culture management:** Monitor employee attrition as a leading indicator of organizational health.
- **Any stock-and-flow system:** Apply whenever you manage a resource that can be both added and lost.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When churn is a feature, not a bug:** Some systems are designed for high turnover (temporary staffing, project-based services) — applying a low-churn framework misses the business model.
- **Short-term snapshots:** Churn rates are meaningful over time, not in a single period. One bad month of attrition may be noise.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Define what is churning:** Customers, employees, members, revenue, energy — be specific.
2. **Measure the churn rate:** What percentage of the existing stock is lost per period?
3. **Calculate the implied lifetime:** 1 ÷ churn rate = average retention period.
4. **Identify the break-even acquisition rate:** What inflow is needed just to maintain current levels?
5. **Trace churn to its causes:** Segment churned elements and identify patterns — what triggered the exit?
6. **Fix the leak before pouring more in:** Reducing churn yields compounding returns; adding more acquisition without fixing churn is expensive and temporary.
7. **Monitor leading indicators:** Often churn can be predicted early (engagement drop, complaint patterns) — intervene before loss.
