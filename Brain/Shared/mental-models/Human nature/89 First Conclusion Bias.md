# First Conclusion Bias

## Description

**First Conclusion Bias** is the tendency to anchor on and then defend the first satisfying explanation that comes to mind, stopping the search for better alternatives prematurely. Once a hypothesis feels plausible, the mind shifts from inquiry mode to confirmation mode — gathering supporting evidence rather than testing whether the conclusion is actually correct.

- **Origins:** Charlie Munger cites this as one of the most dangerous cognitive biases, quoting Josh Billings: "It ain't what you don't know that gets you into trouble. It's what you know for sure that just ain't so." In psychology, it overlaps with the anchoring effect (Kahneman and Tversky), the "satisficing" behavior described by Herbert Simon (1955), and the confirmation bias literature.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **The satisficing trap:** The mind is not designed to find the best explanation — it is designed to find a good-enough one quickly. Once a plausible explanation is found, cognitive effort is redirected to defending it rather than continuing to search.
    - **Confirmation mode:** After the first conclusion, subsequent information processing becomes asymmetric — supporting evidence is readily accepted; disconfirming evidence is subjected to higher scrutiny or explained away.
    - **The cost of early closure:** First conclusions are based on the least information available — early in the analysis, when data is most limited and the mind is least informed. Premature closure locks in the worst-informed judgment.
    - **Expert intuition can worsen the bias:** Experts with pattern recognition from experience may reach first conclusions very quickly and with high confidence — both of which entrench the bias.
    - **The remedy — active search for alternatives:** Deliberately generate multiple competing explanations before selecting any. Force the search to continue even after a plausible answer is found.

- **Real-World Examples:** A doctor who diagnoses a patient based on the first symptom pattern recognized and stops gathering more information — classic diagnostic error. An investor who hears a compelling thesis and then researches it to confirm rather than to test. A police detective who identifies a suspect early and builds the case around them rather than following all evidence.

---

## When to Use

- **Any diagnostic or analytical task:** Before accepting a conclusion, explicitly generate at least two competing explanations and evaluate them against the evidence.
- **High-stakes decisions:** Apply deliberate "devil's advocate" thinking — assign someone to attack the first conclusion before it becomes the only conclusion.
- **Post-mortem and root cause analysis:** Require that multiple hypotheses be entertained before settling on an explanation for a failure.
- **Evaluating creative work:** Before accepting the first solution to a design problem, generate several alternatives — the first solution is rarely the best one.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Time-critical situations:** In fast-moving crises, acting on a first plausible conclusion may be correct. The cost of delayed action can exceed the cost of an imperfect conclusion.
- **When sufficient evidence already exists:** If the first conclusion is based on extensive pre-existing evidence rather than in-the-moment anchoring, continued search may be inefficient.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Notice the first conclusion:** What explanation immediately felt satisfying or obvious?
2. **Pause before accepting it:** Treat the first conclusion as a hypothesis, not a conclusion.
3. **Generate alternatives:** Force yourself to develop at least two or three competing explanations. How else could this situation be explained?
4. **Evaluate all hypotheses against the evidence:** What evidence supports each alternative? What evidence contradicts each?
5. **Seek the most falsifiable test:** What evidence would most clearly distinguish between the competing explanations? Find it.
6. **Accept uncertainty:** If the evidence doesn't clearly favor one explanation, hold multiple hypotheses rather than forcing closure.
7. **Review first conclusions under new information:** As new data arrives, revisit your first conclusion rather than automatically filtering it through confirmation.
