# Tendency to Distort Due to Liking or Loving

## Description

**Tendency to Distort Due to Liking or Loving** is the bias where our positive feelings for a person, organization, or idea systematically skew our perception of their qualities, reasoning, and actions. When we like or love someone, we unconsciously attribute positive characteristics to them (the halo effect) and process their actions and arguments with less critical scrutiny than we apply to strangers or adversaries.

- **Origins:** The halo effect was first documented by psychologist Edward Thorndike in 1920 — soldiers rated high on one trait (physical appearance) were systematically rated higher on all other traits. Charlie Munger extensively discusses love bias in "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" as one of the most dangerous distortions, particularly in investment decisions ("falling in love with a stock") and in hiring decisions (over-rating candidates you personally like).

- **Key Principles:**
    - **The halo effect:** One positive quality radiates to all others. An attractive person is assumed to be more intelligent, moral, and capable. A charismatic leader is assumed to have a sound strategy.
    - **Motivated reasoning in relationships:** When we love someone, we unconsciously seek evidence that confirms their goodness and dismiss or reframe evidence of their flaws.
    - **Love bias in investing:** Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger warn that investors who "fall in love" with a stock begin defending its price rather than evaluating it. Selling feels like betrayal.
    - **Loyalty and distortion:** Long-term loyalty — to a person, brand, team, or idea — creates an accumulated investment that makes objective re-evaluation psychologically costly.
    - **Symmetry — hate also distorts:** The inverse is equally powerful: we undervalue and unfairly criticize those we dislike. "Distortion from liking or loving" and "distortion from hating or disliking" are mirror biases.

- **Real-World Examples:** A manager consistently giving higher performance reviews to employees they personally like, regardless of objective performance. An investor holding a losing position because they "believe in" the company's founder. A board failing to act on a CEO's declining performance because of personal loyalty built over years. A parent consistently overestimating a child's abilities and defending their failures.

---

## When to Use

- **Investment and business decisions:** Explicitly examine whether your positive feeling for an investment or person is distorting your evaluation.
- **Hiring and promotion:** Use structured evaluation criteria applied before meeting candidates to reduce halo effects from likability.
- **Partnership and relationship assessment:** Periodically evaluate important relationships with explicit criteria independent of your feelings.
- **Evaluating your own advocates' arguments:** When an argument is made by someone you respect or like, apply the same scrutiny you'd give a skeptic.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Overriding all emotion in relationships:** Love bias is a distortion but love itself is valuable. The goal is calibration, not emotional detachment.
- **Assuming positive feelings always corrupt judgment:** Familiarity and positive regard can also generate genuine insight about a person or situation, not just distortion.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the love or liking:** Do you have significant positive feelings for the person, organization, or idea being evaluated?
2. **Apply the halo test:** Are you attributing positive qualities beyond what you have direct evidence for?
3. **Check for asymmetric scrutiny:** Are you applying less critical scrutiny to this party's arguments than you would apply to a neutral party's?
4. **Deliberately seek disconfirming evidence:** Force yourself to find the strongest case against the position you're inclined to accept.
5. **Use objective criteria:** Define evaluation criteria in advance, before positive feelings can influence which criteria seem most important.
6. **Consult an independent party:** Ask someone with no positive feelings for the subject to evaluate it and compare their assessment to yours.
7. **Separate the person from the evaluation:** You can like the person and still evaluate their work, reasoning, or proposal independently.
