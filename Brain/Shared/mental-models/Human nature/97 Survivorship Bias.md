# Survivorship Bias

## Description

**Survivorship Bias** is the logical error of focusing on entities that passed a selection process while overlooking those that did not — typically because the failures are invisible. By studying only survivors, we draw systematically misleading conclusions about what produces success, because the failures that would contradict our hypotheses are absent from the data.

- **Origins:** The canonical example comes from mathematician Abraham Wald's WWII analysis of aircraft damage. Military engineers proposed reinforcing the areas of returned planes that showed the most bullet holes. Wald pointed out the error: the planes that returned showed where hits could be survived. The planes that didn't return — invisible in the data — showed where hits were fatal. Reinforce the areas with no damage on returning planes. The concept was later popularized by Nassim Taleb and Michael Shermer.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **The missing data is the signal:** Survivorship bias is invisible by definition — you only see what survived. The most important information is often in what you can't see.
    - **Success stories as biased samples:** Successful companies, investors, people, and strategies are a massively biased sample of all attempts. The same approach that produced the successes you see also produced failures you don't.
    - **The self-help industry runs on survivorship bias:** Advice from successful people describes what worked for survivors — an unrepresentative sample of everyone who tried those approaches.
    - **Cemetery logic:** Imagine asking survivors of a battlefield what saved them. Each has a different answer — the true answer is a combination of skill and luck, but the failures who could reveal luck's role are absent.
    - **The fix — seek the denominator:** Before drawing conclusions from a set of successes, ask: how many people/companies/strategies attempted this? What happened to the rest?

- **Real-World Examples:** The myth that Harvard dropouts become billionaires — without knowing how many dropouts from all universities attempted entrepreneurship and failed, the success rate is unknown. Investment strategies that "worked" — without knowing how many similar strategies failed. Famous authors who say "rejection made me stronger" — the equally-rejected authors who gave up aren't telling their stories.

---

## When to Use

- **Learning from success:** Before applying lessons from successful examples, ask: what is the full distribution of outcomes for this approach? What happened to those who tried and failed?
- **Investment and strategy analysis:** Evaluate strategies based on their full track record, not just the visible winners.
- **Self-help and advice evaluation:** When advice comes from successful people, ask: how many people followed this approach? What happened to the majority?
- **Research methodology:** In any study, explicitly account for what is missing from the sample and why.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When survival itself is the selection criterion of interest:** Sometimes you specifically want to study what survivors did — if you're studying long-lived companies specifically to understand longevity, survivorship is the variable of interest.
- **When failure data is genuinely unavailable:** You can't always get the denominator. In those cases, acknowledge the limitation rather than pretending it doesn't affect your conclusions.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the visible sample:** What entities, strategies, or outcomes are you observing?
2. **Ask who is missing:** What attempts, failures, or non-survivors are absent from this sample?
3. **Estimate the denominator:** How many entities tried the approach that produced these visible successes?
4. **Calculate the base rate:** Success count ÷ total attempts = actual success rate. Is this consistent with the impression you'd get from survivors alone?
5. **Identify selection mechanisms:** What process selected for who survived into your sample? What biases does this create?
6. **Seek failure data:** Actively look for evidence of failures — case studies of failed companies, investors who used the same strategy and lost, studies that track all participants rather than just successes.
7. **Adjust conclusions:** Weight your lessons and predictions against the full distribution, not just the visible successes.
