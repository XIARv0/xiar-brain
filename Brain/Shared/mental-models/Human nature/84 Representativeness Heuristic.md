# Representativeness Heuristic

## Description

**The Representativeness Heuristic** is the cognitive shortcut of judging probability by how closely something resembles a prototype or stereotype — by how "representative" it is of a category — rather than by actual statistical probability. This produces systematic errors, most famously the conjunction fallacy (adding details makes something feel more probable) and the neglect of base rates.

- **Origins:** Documented by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in their 1972 paper, formalized with the famous "Linda Problem" (1983): Linda is described as a feminist, socially concerned philosophy graduate. People judged it more probable that Linda is "a bank teller and active in the feminist movement" than "a bank teller" — mathematically impossible, but representativeness of the feminist archetype overrides logical probability.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **The conjunction fallacy:** Adding details that increase representativeness makes scenarios feel more probable, even though adding constraints always makes them statistically less probable.
    - **Base rate neglect:** When something matches a prototype strongly, people ignore the base rate probability of that category. A description that sounds like a serial killer leads people to estimate high probability of criminality regardless of how rare serial killers actually are.
    - **Stereotyping:** The representativeness heuristic is the mechanism behind stereotyping — we judge individuals by how well they match category prototypes rather than by individual probability distributions.
    - **The narrative pulls:** Detailed, coherent, internally consistent stories feel more probable than abstract probabilities because they are representative of how we understand causality — but they are actually less probable for being specific.
    - **Diagnostic reasoning errors:** Doctors who diagnose by representativeness (this patient looks like a typical case of X) without checking base rates make systematic errors, especially for rare conditions that mimic common ones.

- **Real-World Examples:** Investors who mistake a "great story" stock for a probable investment — a compelling narrative doesn't make the outcome more likely. Hiring managers who select candidates based on how well they match the "typical successful employee" prototype rather than predictive criteria. Medical misdiagnosis when a presentation strongly resembles a common condition and a rare condition is missed.

---

## When to Use

- **Evaluating narratives and stories:** When a scenario feels highly probable because it's coherent and detailed, explicitly check the statistical base rates.
- **Hiring and selection:** Replace prototype-matching with structured, criterion-based evaluation that doesn't rely on "does this person look the part?"
- **Medical and diagnostic reasoning:** Always check base rates alongside pattern-matching — how common is this condition in patients presenting this way?
- **Investment analysis:** Separate the quality of the story from the probability of the outcome — a compelling narrative does not increase the base rate probability of success.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When representativeness reflects genuine category membership:** Sometimes looking like a duck and quacking like a duck means it's a duck. Pattern recognition from representativeness is often accurate.
- **When base rates are unavailable:** If no reliable base rate exists, representativeness may be the best available heuristic — just hold the resulting estimate with appropriate uncertainty.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the judgment:** What probability or category membership are you estimating?
2. **Identify the prototype being invoked:** What mental template or stereotype is making this seem highly probable?
3. **Check base rate:** What is the actual frequency of this category in the relevant population?
4. **Apply Bayes' Theorem intuitively:** Even a highly representative match requires a high prior probability to produce a high posterior probability.
5. **Examine the story for conjunction:** Are you adding details that increase plausibility but actually reduce statistical probability?
6. **Separate narrative quality from probability:** A compelling story does not increase the underlying probability of the outcome.
7. **Seek base rate anchors:** Before making a judgment, explicitly ground it in base rate statistics rather than representativeness alone.
