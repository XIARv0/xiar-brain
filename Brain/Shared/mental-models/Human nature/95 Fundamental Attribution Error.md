# Fundamental Attribution Error

## Description

**The Fundamental Attribution Error (FAE)** is the pervasive tendency to over-attribute other people's behavior to their character or disposition while under-attributing it to situational factors — and simultaneously doing the opposite for our own behavior (over-attributing our actions to circumstances). It leads to harsh, inaccurate judgments of others and miscalibrated predictions of behavior.

- **Origins:** Named and formalized by social psychologist Lee Ross in 1977, drawing on earlier attribution research by Fritz Heider, Harold Kelley, and Edward Jones. The actor-observer asymmetry (Jones and Nisbett, 1972) describes the directional bias: actors explain their own behavior situationally ("I was tired"); observers explain the same behavior dispositionally ("they're irresponsible").

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Dispositional vs. situational attribution:** The FAE overweights stable personal traits and underweights situational pressures, incentives, constraints, and context when explaining behavior.
    - **The actor-observer asymmetry:** The same behavior is explained differently depending on whether you're the actor or the observer. Actors see their situation; observers see the person.
    - **Cultural variation:** The FAE is stronger in individualistic Western cultures than in collectivist cultures, where situational and relational explanations of behavior are more salient.
    - **The power of situation:** The Stanford Prison Experiment, Milgram's obedience studies, and decades of social psychology research demonstrate that situation is far more predictive of behavior than people assume.
    - **Practical implications:** Better predictions of behavior come from understanding the situation a person is in (incentives, constraints, norms, pressure) than from assessing their character in isolation. This is why good managers change systems, not just people.

- **Real-World Examples:** A manager concluding that a team member's subpar performance reflects poor work ethic when it actually reflects an unclear brief and inadequate resources. A hiring manager attributing an interview performance to a candidate's personality when it was driven by the interview setting's stress and structure. Cross-cultural misunderstandings where behavior driven by cultural norms is attributed to individual character.

---

## When to Use

- **Performance management:** Before attributing poor performance to disposition, audit the situational factors — clarity of expectations, resources, feedback, organizational support.
- **Conflict analysis:** When an individual or group is behaving in frustrating ways, explicitly examine the situation they're in before concluding it reflects their character.
- **Hiring and assessment:** Recognize that interview performance is heavily situational — design assessment processes that reveal actual capabilities across realistic situations.
- **Systems design:** Recognize that changing the situation (incentives, processes, defaults) changes behavior more reliably than trying to change people.
- **Self-awareness:** Apply the asymmetry to yourself — when you explain your own behavior situationally, apply the same generosity to others.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When character genuinely explains behavior:** Some behavior patterns, consistent across many situations over time, do reflect stable character traits. The FAE identifies a bias — it doesn't mean dispositions never matter.
- **In high-stakes, single-event judgments:** In some decisions (hiring for high-trust roles, partnership selection), character assessment is appropriate even without long track records.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Observe the behavior:** What specific behavior are you trying to explain?
2. **Note your initial attribution:** What is your first instinct — is this about the person or the situation?
3. **Audit the situational factors:** What pressures, incentives, constraints, norms, and context did this person face? What situation were they in?
4. **Apply the actor's perspective:** How would this person explain their own behavior? What situational factors would they point to?
5. **Revise the attribution:** Adjust your explanation to give appropriate weight to situational factors.
6. **Predict behavior situationally:** Instead of "what kind of person is this?", ask "what kind of situation will this person be in?" — the latter predicts behavior better.
7. **Design the situation:** If you want to change behavior, change the situation (incentives, structure, defaults) rather than trying to change the person through exhortation.
