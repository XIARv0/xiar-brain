# Tendency to Want to Do Something (Fight or Flight)

## Description

**Tendency to Want to Do Something (Fight or Flight)** describes the biological stress response that drives action — fight or flee — in response to threats. In modern environments, this ancient response manifests as a powerful impulse to do something when faced with uncertainty, bad news, or apparent threats, even when the optimal response is deliberate inaction. Commission bias — acting when you shouldn't — is one of the most costly and underappreciated failure modes in decision-making.

- **Origins:** Walter Cannon described the fight-or-flight response in 1915 as the sympathetic nervous system's mobilization for physical threat. In decision science, commission bias (tendency to act rather than not act) was documented by Ritov and Baron (1992). Daniel Kahneman's System 1/System 2 framework describes how the fast, reactive system (which drives fight-or-flight) overrides the slow, deliberate system under stress.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Action feels like control:** Doing something — anything — reduces anxiety because it creates a sense of agency. Inaction feels passive and helpless, even when it is the rational choice.
    - **Commission vs. omission bias:** People rate harms caused by action as worse than equivalent harms caused by inaction — creating pressure to act to avoid the psychological sting of "not trying."
    - **The cost of premature action:** In medical, military, financial, and organizational contexts, acting too soon on insufficient information frequently causes more damage than the original threat. The "do something" impulse overrides the diagnostic patience needed to act correctly.
    - **Noise trading and over-management:** Investors who trade too frequently destroy returns with transaction costs and poor timing. Managers who constantly intervene prevent teams from developing autonomous capability.
    - **Creating space for deliberate response:** The antidote is not suppression of the impulse but the creation of systematic pause mechanisms — cooling-off periods, structured decision frameworks, explicit "do nothing" options.

- **Real-World Examples:** The medical maxim "when in doubt, don't" — aggressive intervention in uncertain cases often causes more harm than watchful waiting. Investment portfolios managed by passive systems (index funds) consistently outperform actively managed ones, partly because the impulse to act is removed. Military commanders who respond to provocations with escalation rather than restraint, turning manageable incidents into wars.

---

## When to Use

- **Crisis management:** Explicitly build in pause before acting in crisis situations — distinguish between genuine emergencies requiring immediate action and anxious responses to uncertainty.
- **Investment and financial decisions:** Recognize the impulse to act in volatile markets as the fight-or-flight response and build systematic friction against impulsive trading.
- **Over-management diagnosis:** Identify whether frequent interventions are driven by genuine necessity or by the discomfort of inaction.
- **Medical and technical decision-making:** Cultivate "watchful waiting" as a legitimate, valued option alongside active intervention.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When genuine inaction is catastrophic:** Some situations genuinely require immediate action and delay is the dangerous path. Distinguish real emergencies from anxiety-driven false ones.
- **Chronic inaction disguised as deliberation:** The opposite bias — paralysis by analysis — is also a failure mode. The goal is calibrated response timing, not defaulting to inaction.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Notice the impulse:** Are you feeling a strong drive to act? Is this impulse driven by genuine analysis or by discomfort with uncertainty?
2. **Pause deliberately:** Build in a mandatory pause — even 60 seconds — before acting in response to a stressful stimulus.
3. **Classify the urgency:** Is this a genuine emergency where delay causes irreversible harm? Or is it a situation where a few minutes of thinking wouldn't cost anything?
4. **Evaluate the "do nothing" option explicitly:** What happens if you take no action? Is inaction genuinely worse than premature action?
5. **Gather minimum required information:** Identify the information that would most change your action, and spend the shortest reasonable time gathering it before acting.
6. **Separate anxiety from analysis:** "I feel I must do something" is not an argument for action. Separate the feeling from the decision.
7. **Design friction against impulse:** In domains where you know the act-impulse is dangerous (trading, escalation, confrontation), build systematic delays and approval requirements.
