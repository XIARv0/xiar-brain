# Availability Heuristic

## Description

**The Availability Heuristic** is the mental shortcut by which people estimate the probability or frequency of events based on how easily examples come to mind — how "available" they are cognitively. Because recent, vivid, emotionally charged, and widely reported events are more cognitively available, they are systematically overweighted in probability estimates relative to their actual base rates.

- **Origins:** Identified by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1973, the availability heuristic was one of the first documented cognitive shortcuts (heuristics) that produce systematic predictable errors. Their research showed that people judge the frequency of words containing "k" in the third position as lower than words starting with "k" — because words starting with "k" are easier to retrieve, even though the former are more common.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Vividness inflates probability:** Dramatic, emotional events (plane crashes, shark attacks, terrorism) are overestimated in probability because they are memorable and widely reported. Common but unexciting causes of death (heart disease, car accidents) are underestimated.
    - **Recency bias:** Recent events are more cognitively available, so recent experience dominates probability estimates even when it's a small, unrepresentative sample.
    - **Media and availability:** News media systematically selects for dramatic, unusual events — creating availability that drives massive overestimation of rare but dramatic risks.
    - **Personal experience as availability sample:** Your own experience — a tiny, biased sample — is the most cognitively available data you have, so it dramatically over-influences your probability estimates.
    - **Correcting with base rates:** The corrective to availability bias is base rate statistics — looking up the actual frequency of an event rather than relying on cognitive availability.

- **Real-World Examples:** After 9/11, many Americans drove rather than flew, despite driving being statistically far more dangerous — plane crashes were more cognitively available. Investors overweight recent market events (a crash makes future crashes seem more likely). Doctors who recently saw a rare disease are more likely to diagnose it in the next patient, regardless of actual prevalence.

---

## When to Use

- **Risk assessment:** When evaluating risks, explicitly look up base rates rather than relying on intuitive probability estimates.
- **Decision-making after vivid events:** After a dramatic recent event, deliberately correct for the availability inflation it creates in probability estimates.
- **Evaluating media narratives:** Recognize that news coverage selects for unusual events — the sample you're seeing is heavily biased toward dramatic, availability-inflating cases.
- **Investment decisions:** After a recent market crash or surge, explicitly examine whether your probability estimates are being distorted by availability of recent events.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When availability reflects genuine patterns:** Sometimes frequently mentioned events are mentioned frequently because they genuinely occur frequently. Not all cognitively available information is bias-distorted.
- **Over-correcting:** Being aware of availability bias shouldn't make you dismiss vivid, memorable information entirely — it may still carry genuine signal.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the probability estimate:** What frequency or likelihood are you estimating?
2. **Assess cognitive availability:** What examples of this event come to mind easily? Why — because they're common, or because they're vivid/recent?
3. **Look up the base rate:** What does empirical data say about the actual frequency of this event?
4. **Identify the gap:** Is your intuitive estimate significantly higher or lower than the base rate? Which direction is it biased?
5. **Correct for media and recency:** Has your estimate been inflated by recent news coverage or recent personal experience of a dramatic event?
6. **Make the calibrated estimate:** Weight your probability estimate toward the base rate, with adjustment for genuine new information only.
7. **Track your calibration:** Over time, compare your probability estimates to outcomes to build better-calibrated intuitions.
