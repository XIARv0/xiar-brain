# Commitment and Consistency Bias

## Description

**Commitment and Consistency Bias** is the powerful psychological tendency to behave in ways consistent with prior commitments — even when circumstances have changed and the original commitment is no longer rational. People feel strong social and internal pressure to "be consistent" with what they have previously said or done, making them resistant to changing course even when new information clearly warrants it.

- **Origins:** Robert Cialdini documented commitment and consistency as one of his six principles of influence in *Influence* (1984). The psychological mechanism is cognitive dissonance reduction (Leon Festinger, 1957) — inconsistency between behavior and belief creates uncomfortable psychological tension that people resolve by changing their beliefs to match their behavior rather than changing their behavior.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Public commitment amplifies the bias:** Once a commitment is made publicly, the social stakes of inconsistency rise. People resist changing publicly stated positions far more than private ones.
    - **The foot-in-the-door technique:** Getting people to agree to small requests increases compliance with larger ones — each small commitment creates consistency pressure to comply with the next.
    - **Escalation of commitment (sunk cost escalation):** Prior investment creates consistency pressure to continue — "we've come this far" rationalizes further investment even when forward-looking analysis would not support it.
    - **Self-perception theory (Bem):** People partly learn their own attitudes by observing their own behavior — if I did X, I must believe Y. Commitments thus actually change beliefs over time.
    - **The virtue of graceful updating:** The ability to change position in response to evidence — without social penalty — requires explicitly cultivating "intellectual honesty" as a valued social norm. "I was wrong and here is what changed my mind" should be praised, not punished.

- **Real-World Examples:** Politicians who maintain positions they no longer believe in because changing them is politically costly. Investors who hold losing positions because selling would mean admitting the original decision was wrong. Organizations that continue failed strategies because executives who championed them are still in power. A person who stays in a deteriorating relationship because they've publicly committed to it.

---

## When to Use

- **Diagnosing escalation:** When a project or investment is continuing despite mounting evidence of failure, identify whether consistency bias is the hidden driver.
- **Influence strategy:** Use small initial commitments (foot-in-the-door) strategically to build larger commitments.
- **Pre-commitment:** Deliberately make public commitments to desired behaviors — the consistency pressure then works for you.
- **Evaluating your own resistance to change:** When you find yourself defending a position strongly, ask whether you're defending the evidence or defending your prior commitment.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Confusing with legitimate persistence:** Not all consistency is bias — some commitments should be maintained despite opposition (core values, long-term strategies). Distinguish principled consistency from fear of inconsistency.
- **Exploiting it manipulatively:** Using commitment and consistency to trap people into escalating commitments against their genuine interests is manipulation.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the prior commitment:** What did you say, do, or invest in that is creating consistency pressure?
2. **Separate the commitment from the current evidence:** What would you decide if you had no prior commitment and were evaluating this situation fresh?
3. **Assess the consistency gap:** How much is your current position driven by new evidence vs. desire to be consistent with the past?
4. **Calculate forward-looking value:** What are the future costs and benefits of continuing vs. stopping, independent of sunk costs?
5. **Practice graceful updating:** Frame position changes as evidence of intellectual honesty, not weakness: "I was wrong for these reasons, and here's what I now believe."
6. **Create exit ramps in advance:** When making commitments, define in advance the conditions under which you will reverse them — pre-empting consistency pressure with pre-committed criteria for change.
7. **Reduce public commitment to tentative positions:** For decisions still under evaluation, avoid public statements that will create consistency pressure before you have adequate information.
