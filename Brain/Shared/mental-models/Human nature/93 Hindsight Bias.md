# Hindsight Bias

## Description

**Hindsight Bias** is the "I knew it all along" phenomenon — after an event occurs, people systematically overestimate how predictable and foreseeable it was. The known outcome retroactively restructures how we remember our uncertainty, making what was genuinely unpredictable feel inevitable in retrospect. This corrupts learning, performance evaluation, and historical understanding.

- **Origins:** Baruch Fischhoff identified and named hindsight bias in 1975 through experiments where people who learned an event's outcome consistently reported they "would have" predicted it before knowing. Daniel Kahneman incorporated it as one of the most important biases in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011). Nassim Taleb's concept of the "narrative fallacy" is closely related — we construct post-hoc stories that make outcomes seem inevitable.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Memory reconstruction:** Humans don't store memories like recordings — we reconstruct them at recall. Knowing the outcome influences how the past is reconstructed, making it seem like the outcome was always clearly indicated.
    - **Creeping determinism:** The feeling that what happened was inevitable grows progressively once the outcome is known. The uncertainty that actually existed before the fact disappears from memory.
    - **Corrupts performance evaluation:** A decision that led to a bad outcome feels in retrospect like a bad decision — even if it was the rational choice given available information at the time. This conflates outcome quality with decision quality.
    - **Prevents learning:** If bad outcomes feel like they "should have been obvious," the response is punishment and attribution of blame rather than systematic analysis of what information was available and what process was used.
    - **The remedy — prospective records:** Recording predictions and reasoning in writing before events occur provides the only reliable defense against hindsight bias. Retrospective assessments are inherently contaminated.

- **Real-World Examples:** The 2008 financial crisis is now described as obviously foreseeable — but at the time, the majority of professional forecasters, regulators, and investors did not predict it. "Of course [competitor] was going to win" said after the competitive outcome. Medical errors evaluated after harm are judged as obviously negligent; the same decisions made without adverse outcome are considered acceptable care.

---

## When to Use

- **Performance reviews and post-mortems:** Explicitly counteract hindsight bias by reconstructing what information was available at the decision time, not what is known now.
- **Forecasting calibration:** Record predictions before events to create an honest record for future calibration, immune to hindsight revision.
- **Legal and regulatory judgment:** Evaluate decisions based on what a reasonable person knowing what the actor knew at the time would do — not what seems obvious post-outcome.
- **Learning from history:** Be skeptical of historical accounts that make past events seem inevitable — the uncertainty that existed at the time is systematically obscured.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When outcomes genuinely were predictable:** Sometimes a bad outcome was the result of obviously bad decision-making. Hindsight bias doesn't mean all bad decisions looked reasonable ex ante.
- **In simple, well-understood domains:** Where causal relationships are clear and well-understood, post-outcome evaluation is more reliable.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the outcome:** What happened that you (or others) are now evaluating?
2. **Reconstruct the ex ante information:** What information was actually available before the outcome? List it explicitly.
3. **Identify what was genuinely uncertain:** What aspects of the outcome were not predictable from the available information?
4. **Separate decision quality from outcome quality:** Was the decision reasonable given the available information, regardless of the outcome?
5. **Record prospectively:** For future important decisions, write down your reasoning and predictions before the outcome occurs.
6. **Apply consistently:** Evaluate both good and bad outcomes for hindsight bias — "that was obviously brilliant" is as susceptible as "that was obviously stupid."
7. **Use pre-mortems:** Before important decisions, imagine they've failed and ask "what might we have missed?" — reducing hindsight's blinding effect by forcing prospective uncertainty consideration.
