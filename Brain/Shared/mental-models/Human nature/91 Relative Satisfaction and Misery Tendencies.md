# Relative Satisfaction and Misery Tendencies

## Description

**Relative Satisfaction and Misery Tendencies** describes the empirical finding that human happiness and suffering are determined primarily by comparison — to past states, to peers, and to expectations — rather than by absolute conditions. The hedonic treadmill means that achieving goals rarely produces lasting satisfaction, because the comparison baseline adjusts. Applied cognitively, it explains why wealth, achievement, and status don't reliably produce wellbeing, and why reference point management is a key skill.

- **Origins:** Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell coined the "hedonic treadmill" concept in 1971, showing that people adapt quickly to improvements in their circumstances and return to baseline happiness. Lottery winners and paralysis patients both return near their prior happiness levels within months. Leon Festinger's social comparison theory (1954) documented that humans evaluate their own conditions relative to those of comparable others.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Hedonic adaptation:** Humans rapidly adapt to new positive circumstances — the excitement of a raise, a new relationship, or a new house fades as it becomes the new baseline. What was desired becomes neutral; the desire moves forward.
    - **The hedonic treadmill:** Each achievement shifts the comparison baseline upward. You must keep running (achieving more) just to maintain the same level of satisfaction — precisely mirroring the Red Queen Effect.
    - **Social comparison:** Happiness is powerfully influenced by comparison to peers. A 10% raise feels great if peers received 5% and awful if they received 20% — the absolute amount is secondary to the relative position.
    - **Relative deprivation:** People feel poorer relative to rising societal wealth even if their absolute wealth has increased. The sense of inadequacy comes from comparison, not absolute deprivation.
    - **The reference point:** Charlie Munger: "The best way to get what you want is to deserve what you want." But the model here adds: the best way to be satisfied with what you have is to manage your comparison baseline.

- **Real-World Examples:** Employees who receive market-rate salaries but discover peers earn more experience dissatisfaction despite objectively good pay. Countries with increasing GDP but increasing inequality report declining life satisfaction. Professional athletes who earn $5M/year but compare to $50M peers feel underpaid. The wellbeing research consistently shows that above ~$75,000/year (now ~$100K), additional income provides negligible additional happiness.

---

## When to Use

- **Personal wellbeing:** Deliberately manage your comparison baselines — compare to your own past rather than peers at higher levels, and practice gratitude for what you have.
- **Compensation and incentive design:** Understand that relative pay is more motivating than absolute pay; salary information transparency affects morale through comparison effects.
- **Goal-setting:** Distinguish between goals that will produce genuine lasting change (relationships, meaningful work, health) and goals that will produce hedonic adaptation (possessions, status).
- **Evaluating satisfaction data:** Satisfaction surveys measure relative perceptions, not absolute conditions — interpret them in context.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Dismissing genuine needs:** Relative satisfaction theory doesn't mean absolute deprivation doesn't matter — it does. Below basic needs thresholds, absolute conditions dominate.
- **Using it to justify unfair relative positions:** The fact that satisfaction is relative doesn't mean unfair comparative outcomes are acceptable.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify your comparison baseline:** What reference point are you comparing your current situation to?
2. **Question the comparison's validity:** Is this the appropriate reference point? Are you comparing to the right peers, the right time frame?
3. **Apply hedonic adaptation awareness:** When pursuing a goal, explicitly anticipate that achievement will produce less lasting satisfaction than predicted — plan for what comes after.
4. **Practice downward comparison:** Compare your current situation to your past or to those less fortunate — this is a documented intervention for increasing experienced satisfaction.
5. **Seek absolute wellbeing anchors:** Identify aspects of your life that are genuinely good regardless of comparison — health, relationships, meaningful work — and cultivate attention to them.
6. **Design incentives with comparison in mind:** In organizational contexts, consider how relative comparisons will shape perceptions of reward systems.
7. **Set post-achievement goals:** Because achievement adapts, plan the next meaningful goal before reaching the current one — channeling the treadmill constructively.
