# Tendency to Feel Envy and Jealousy

## Description

**Envy and Jealousy** are hardwired social emotions that powerfully distort judgment and drive behavior, often destructively. Envy is wanting what others have; jealousy is the fear of losing what you have to a rival. Both evolved as social monitoring mechanisms, but in modern environments they cause systematic poor decisions — in investing, careers, relationships, and life satisfaction.

- **Origins:** Evolutionary psychologists argue that envy and jealousy evolved to motivate status-seeking and protect vital resources and relationships. In moral philosophy, envy is often called the most destructive of the seven deadly sins — uniquely providing no pleasure even to the person experiencing it. Charlie Munger frequently cites envy as one of the most dangerous biases: "It's not greed that drives the world, but envy."

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Envy is comparison-based, not absolute:** You are not envious of someone impossibly beyond you (a billionaire when you're middle class) but of those close to you — peers, colleagues, neighbors. The closer the comparison, the sharper the envy.
    - **Envy drives irrational decisions:** Envy motivates actions that harm the envied party even at cost to yourself — a defining feature. In the Ultimatum Game, people reject profitable offers they perceive as unfair to punish the "winner."
    - **The pain without pleasure:** Unlike greed (which at least produces pleasure when satisfied), envy produces pain — from others' success — without producing comparable pleasure from their failure. It is a pure negative.
    - **Jealousy as threat response:** Romantic jealousy and professional jealousy both activate threat systems — reducing cognition and increasing defensive, sometimes aggressive behavior.
    - **The antidote — abundance mindset:** Genuinely believing that others' success does not reduce your own — that success is not zero-sum — dissolves envy. Gratitude for your own circumstances reorients the comparison baseline.

- **Real-World Examples:** Investors who take on more risk than is rational because neighbors made money in speculative assets. Employees who undermine colleagues whose success triggers envy rather than learning from them. The market-wide herding behavior in financial bubbles is partly envied gain driving irrational participation.

---

## When to Use

- **Diagnosing your own decision motivation:** When considering a financial or career decision, ask whether envy of someone else's outcome is driving it rather than its own merits.
- **Evaluating others' behavior:** When someone appears to be acting irrationally at cost to themselves, envy or jealousy may be the hidden motive.
- **Organizational design:** Structuring compensation and recognition to reduce intra-team comparison reduces destructive envy dynamics.
- **Relationship design:** In competitive environments, explicitly reframe success as non-zero-sum — celebrate others' wins as team wins rather than relative losses.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Pathologizing normal competitive motivation:** Not all competitive desire is envy. Aspiration to achieve what others have achieved — with genuine admiration rather than resentment — is healthy motivation.
- **Suppressing rather than examining:** Trying to not feel envy without understanding it is ineffective. The solution is to examine the comparison basis and question whether it is valid.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Notice the envy signal:** Recognize the feeling of resentment at others' success or the desire to diminish their gains.
2. **Identify the comparison:** Who are you comparing yourself to? Why this specific person?
3. **Examine the zero-sum assumption:** Are you assuming their success reduces your own? Is this actually true?
4. **Separate the signal from the noise:** Is their success telling you something useful about what's possible? Or are you in different enough circumstances that the comparison is invalid?
5. **Redirect to inspiration:** Can you use their success as evidence of what is achievable and learn from their path?
6. **Practice gratitude:** Reorient your comparison baseline to your own past rather than others' present.
7. **Check decisions for envy contamination:** Before acting on a strong competitive impulse, verify that the decision's merits stand independently of the envy driver.
