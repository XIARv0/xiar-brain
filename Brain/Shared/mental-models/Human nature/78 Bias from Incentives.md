# Bias from Incentives

## Description

**Bias from Incentives** is Charlie Munger's most frequently cited principle: people's judgment is systematically distorted by their incentives — not through deliberate dishonesty, but through unconscious motivated reasoning. When we have a financial, social, or emotional stake in a particular conclusion, we process evidence selectively and construct rationalizations that lead to the desired conclusion, genuinely believing we have reasoned objectively.

- **Origins:** Munger identified this as one of the twenty-five cognitive biases in his famous "Psychology of Human Misjudgment" speech (1995). The psychological mechanism is motivated reasoning — documented extensively by Ziva Kunda (1990) and later Dan Ariely. Upton Sinclair captured it: "It is difficult to get a man to understand something when his salary depends upon his not understanding it."

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Incentives shape perception, not just action:** The bias is not "I know the truth but I'll lie for money." It is "my incentives have shaped what I actually perceive as true." This makes it both more pervasive and more dangerous.
    - **Professional and institutional capture:** Doctors paid per procedure recommend more procedures. Consultants paid for complex solutions recommend complex solutions. Analysts employed by banks to cover bank stocks are systematically more bullish. The incentive distorts the professional judgment.
    - **The "show me the incentive" rule:** Munger's heuristic: before trusting any advice, analysis, or recommendation, identify the advisor's incentive structure. It predicts their conclusion better than their stated reasoning.
    - **Self-serving bias:** People systematically remember their contributions more than others' and attribute successes to their own skill and failures to bad luck — because these attributions serve their self-image.
    - **The fix:** Separate the analysis from the decision; seek advisors with aligned or neutral incentives; explicitly ask "what would I believe if I had the opposite incentive?"; use pre-commitment to lock in standards before knowing outcomes.

- **Real-World Examples:** Financial advisors recommending high-commission products over lower-cost alternatives. Analysts at investment banks rating the bonds of clients as investment-grade. A manager who hired an employee underweighting that person's performance problems because acknowledging them would reflect poorly on the hiring decision. A doctor over-prescribing because the pharmaceutical company provides speaking fees.

---

## When to Use

- **Evaluating any advice or recommendation:** Identify the advisor's incentive structure before weighting their judgment.
- **Self-auditing your own reasoning:** When reaching a conclusion that aligns with your interests, explicitly examine whether incentives are distorting your analysis.
- **Organizational design:** Structure incentives so that the people responsible for decisions are aligned with the outcomes of those decisions.
- **Due diligence:** Map the incentive structures of all parties in any complex transaction to predict where biased analysis is likely.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Assuming all self-interested parties are dishonest:** Incentive bias is mostly unconscious — it doesn't imply malice. The appropriate response is discounting and verification, not accusation.
- **Over-discounting experts with skin in the game:** Sometimes having incentives aligned with outcomes (a surgeon paid for good outcomes, an investor in their own recommendations) reduces bias.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the incentive:** What does this person or organization gain or lose based on the conclusion they reach?
2. **Map the incentive to the recommendation:** Does the conclusion they're reaching align with their incentive? How strongly?
3. **Seek independent verification:** Find an advisor or source with neutral or opposing incentives and compare the analysis.
4. **Apply to yourself:** What is your stake in the conclusion you're reaching? How might it be distorting your reasoning?
5. **Use the "opposite incentive" test:** Ask: "If I had the opposite incentive, what conclusion would I reach?" The gap reveals the bias.
6. **Pre-commit to standards:** Set evaluation criteria before knowing the outcome to prevent incentive distortion from entering the evaluation.
7. **Design incentive-aligned systems:** Structure compensation, evaluation, and accountability to align individual incentives with desired outcomes.
