# Global and Local Maxima

## Description

**Global and Local Maxima** is a mental model from mathematical optimization describing the difference between the best nearby option (local maximum) and the best possible option across all possibilities (global maximum). Incremental improvement always finds the local maximum; finding the global maximum often requires willingness to temporarily move to a worse position.

- **Origins:** From calculus and optimization theory, where finding the maximum of a function requires distinguishing between local peaks (where the function is higher than neighboring points) and the global peak (the highest point anywhere on the function). Applied to strategy and decision-making by thinkers including Shane Parrish, Paul Graham (in the context of startup strategy), and complexity scientist Stuart Kauffman, who used "fitness landscapes" to describe how species and organizations get trapped on local optima.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Local maximum trap:** Incremental optimization always improves within the current approach. But if you are on the wrong hill, getting better at climbing it takes you further from the global maximum.
    - **The valley between peaks:** To reach a higher peak, you must first descend from your current peak — passing through a valley of temporary worse performance. This is psychologically costly and easily misread as failure.
    - **Fitness landscapes:** Kauffman's metaphor: imagine all possible strategies as a landscape, with the height of each point representing its value. Organisms and organizations climb nearby peaks through selection/optimization, but often get stranded on local optima surrounded by lower ground.
    - **Exploration vs. exploitation:** Exploitation (optimizing your current peak) is efficient but myopic. Exploration (searching for higher peaks elsewhere) is costly but necessary when local maxima are suboptimal.
    - **Radical pivots:** Sometimes the only way to reach the global maximum is a non-incremental jump — changing industries, abandoning a product, rewriting from scratch, changing your life's direction.

- **Real-World Examples:** A company that has optimized its business model for fax machines is on a local maximum — further optimization cannot take it to the global maximum of digital communication. A career that has been incrementally optimized within a declining industry. A machine learning model stuck in a local minimum of its loss function, requiring random restarts to escape. A person who has become very good at a life they don't want.

---

## When to Use

- **Strategic pivots:** When growth is stagnating despite excellent execution, ask whether you are on a local maximum and a different direction might be the global one.
- **Career and life decisions:** Distinguish between needing to work harder on your current path (still climbing the local peak) vs. needing to change paths (the current hill isn't the right one).
- **Product and technology strategy:** Recognize when incremental improvement of current technology cannot reach the performance level of a new paradigm.
- **Evaluating commitment:** Before doubling down on a stagnating effort, determine whether you're stuck near the summit or still on the way up.

---

## When to Avoid

- **When you're not yet at the local maximum:** Don't abandon a hill you haven't climbed. Many people escape to a "global search" prematurely, when the real problem is insufficient commitment to the current approach.
- **When the cost of descent is too high:** Sometimes the valley between peaks is too deep (bankruptcy, health crisis, relationship destruction) — the global maximum may not be worth the cost of reaching it.
- **When the landscape is stable:** In mature, well-understood domains, the global maximum is often well-mapped and incremental optimization is the right strategy.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Map your current position:** What strategy, approach, or direction are you currently optimizing?
2. **Assess whether you are still ascending:** Is incremental improvement producing meaningful gains, or have you plateaued?
3. **Question whether you're on the right hill:** Could there be substantially better outcomes from a fundamentally different approach?
4. **Search the landscape:** Survey alternatives — what other peaks might exist? What do the best performers in your domain look like?
5. **Estimate the valley depth:** What would you have to give up temporarily to move toward a potentially higher peak?
6. **Decide: exploit or explore:** Is the evidence for a higher peak strong enough to justify the cost of descent?
7. **If exploring, commit fully:** Half-descending to explore while keeping one foot on the current peak is the worst of both worlds.
8. **Set criteria for returning:** Define in advance what signals would indicate the new peak is actually lower, so you can make a rational decision to return.
