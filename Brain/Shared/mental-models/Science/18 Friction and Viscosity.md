# Friction and Viscosity

## Description

In physics, **friction** is the resistive force that opposes relative motion between two surfaces in contact. **Viscosity** is the analogous property in fluids — a measure of a fluid's resistance to flow. Honey has high viscosity; water has low viscosity. Both concepts capture the same fundamental idea: **resistance embedded within a system that consumes energy and slows movement**.

As mental models, friction and viscosity are among the most practically useful frameworks for analyzing why things move slower than they should — or why they do not move at all.

Key principles:

- **Every system has friction:** No transaction, process, or interaction is frictionless. The question is not whether friction exists but how much of it exists and where it is located. Identifying friction is the first step to reducing it.
- **Friction is often invisible to designers:** The people who build systems are rarely the people who use them under real conditions. Friction accumulates in gaps between assumptions — the extra click, the required field, the approval step — that seem trivial individually but compound into abandonment.
- **Reducing friction accelerates adoption:** Amazon's one-click purchasing, the elimination of passwords via biometrics, and the pre-filled form are all examples of friction removal as competitive strategy. **Every unit of friction removed is a unit of resistance to behavior change eliminated.**
- **Transaction costs are friction:** Ronald Coase's theory of the firm rests on the idea that firms exist to reduce the friction of repeated market transactions. Switching costs — the friction of changing from one vendor to another — create lock-in. **Understanding where friction creates economic value (moats) versus where it destroys it (barriers to adoption) is strategically critical.**
- **Bureaucratic drag is organizational viscosity:** Organizations develop procedural viscosity over time — layers of approval, compliance steps, and coordination requirements that slow decision-making and execution. High-viscosity organizations move slowly even when individuals within them are capable and motivated.
- **Productive friction exists:** Not all friction should be removed. Friction in financial systems (cooling-off periods, confirmation steps) prevents impulsive decisions. **The goal is calibrated friction** — high where it prevents harmful action, low where it impedes beneficial action.

Applied examples: A checkout flow with four screens will convert worse than one with two screens — friction kills conversions. A job application that requires re-entering information already on a resume introduces viscosity that filters out candidates. A regulatory approval process with 18 steps creates organizational viscosity that slows innovation even when no individual step is unreasonable.

---

## When to Use

- **Diagnosing adoption failures:** When a product, process, or behavior change is not being adopted despite genuine value, map the friction points. Friction is often the hidden killer of good ideas.
- **Designing user experiences:** When building any system people interact with, model every step as friction that must be justified by value delivered. Default to removal.
- **Analyzing organizational slowness:** When an organization moves slowly, audit the approval chains, coordination requirements, and procedural steps for viscosity. Often the problem is structural, not motivational.
- **Understanding competitive moats:** When assessing switching costs or market lock-in, quantify the friction that would face a customer switching to a competitor.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Equating all friction with waste:** Some friction is protective. Security checks, contractual review, and ethical oversight all introduce friction that prevents harm. Removing them in the name of efficiency can be catastrophic.
- **Optimizing local friction without considering the system:** Removing friction at one point in a system can move it elsewhere or create new problems. Map the full system before optimizing.
- **Ignoring the cost of friction reduction:** Reducing friction is not free. Engineering time, process redesign, and compliance costs are real. Ensure the benefit of removal exceeds the cost.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Map the full journey:** Identify every step a person or process must take from start to desired outcome. Include steps that seem automatic or trivial — these are where hidden friction lives.

2. **Measure or estimate friction at each step:** What is the time cost, cognitive cost, or emotional cost of each step? Where do people abandon, delay, or make errors?

3. **Classify friction as productive or unproductive:** For each friction point, ask: does this resistance serve a protective or value-generating function, or is it pure drag?

4. **Prioritize high-friction, high-impact points:** Focus removal efforts on the friction points that occur most frequently or at the most consequential moments in the journey.

5. **Design friction removal interventions:** For unproductive friction, brainstorm removal strategies — pre-filling, defaults, automation, simplification, or elimination of steps entirely.

6. **Check for friction displacement:** After removing friction at one point, trace whether it has moved elsewhere in the system. Friction often conserves rather than disappears.

7. **Re-measure after changes:** Friction reduction should produce measurable behavioral change. If it does not, either the friction was not the binding constraint or new friction has emerged.
