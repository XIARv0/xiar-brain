# Ecosystems

## Description

**Ecosystems** is a mental model drawn from ecology, describing a complex network of interdependent actors (organisms, species, companies, ideas) and their environment, where the health of each participant is tied to the health of the whole. No entity exists in isolation; every participant both shapes and is shaped by the system it inhabits.

- **Origins:** The term "ecosystem" was coined by botanist Arthur Tansley in 1935 to describe the web of organisms and their physical environment. Business strategist James Moore popularized the concept for business in 1993, coining "business ecosystem" to describe how companies co-evolve with suppliers, customers, and competitors. Shane Parrish extends it as a general-purpose thinking tool.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Interdependence:** Every actor depends on others — remove one node and ripple effects spread throughout the system.
    - **Co-evolution:** Participants evolve together, each adapting to others' changes, creating emergent complexity.
    - **Resource flows:** Energy, capital, information, and value flow through ecosystems — understanding these flows reveals leverage.
    - **Keystone species:** Some actors disproportionately structure the ecosystem (e.g., Amazon in e-commerce) — their health or removal has outsized effects.
    - **Carrying capacity:** Systems have limits; exceeding them causes collapse.

- **Real-World Examples:** Silicon Valley as a tech ecosystem — universities, VCs, startups, and talent networks all co-evolve. Apple's App Store ecosystem where developers, users, and Apple are mutually dependent. The Amazon rainforest where the loss of one species can cascade to many others.

---

## When to Use

- **Strategic positioning:** Understand your role and dependencies in the ecosystem before making moves.
- **Identifying systemic risks:** When one actor or resource in your ecosystem is fragile, your position may be more vulnerable than it appears.
- **Platform and network strategy:** Build or participate in ecosystems where your presence strengthens others' incentives to stay and participate.
- **Anticipating second-order effects:** Actions that seem local often have ecosystem-wide consequences.
- **Evaluating partnerships:** Assess whether a partnership strengthens or destabilizes the broader ecosystem you both depend on.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Simple bilateral transactions:** When an interaction has no third-party or systemic effects, ecosystem thinking is overhead.
- **Short time horizons:** Ecosystem dynamics play out over long periods — using this model for short-term, urgent decisions may over-complicate them.
- **When you are the ecosystem's keystone:** If your position is dominant, you may underestimate how changes you impose destabilize the system you profit from.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Map the actors:** Identify all key participants — competitors, suppliers, customers, regulators, complementors.
2. **Identify resource flows:** What flows between actors (money, data, talent, attention, goodwill)?
3. **Find the keystones:** Which actors, if removed, would cause the most disruption? Which ones hold the system together?
4. **Assess interdependencies:** How does your success depend on the health of other ecosystem members?
5. **Identify threats to ecosystem health:** What could cause resource depletion, participant exit, or collapse?
6. **Determine your role:** Are you a keystone, a niche player, a competitor, or a complementor?
7. **Act for ecosystem health:** Often the best long-term strategy is to strengthen the ecosystem, not just extract from it.
8. **Monitor for phase transitions:** Ecosystems can shift rapidly — watch for early warning signs of structural change.
