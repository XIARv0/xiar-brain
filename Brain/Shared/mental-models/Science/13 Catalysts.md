# Catalysts

## Description

**Catalysts** is a mental model drawn from chemistry, where a catalyst is a substance that accelerates a chemical reaction without being consumed in the process. It lowers the activation energy required for a reaction to occur, making change faster and more efficient. As a cognitive framework, it describes any element — person, idea, technology, event, or resource — that dramatically speeds up a process or transformation without being depleted itself.

- **Origins:** The chemical concept dates to the early 19th century, formalized by Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius in 1835. As a mental model, Shane Parrish and Charlie Munger adapted it to describe how certain inputs disproportionately accelerate outcomes in business, society, and personal development.

- **Key Principles:**
    - **Acceleration without consumption:** A true catalyst enables change without being used up — a great mentor gives advice repeatedly without losing knowledge.
    - **Lowering barriers:** Catalysts reduce friction, making it easier for a reaction (change, growth, innovation) to happen.
    - **Specificity:** Not all catalysts work for all reactions — the right catalyst must match the right context.

- **Real-World Examples:** A visionary CEO who unlocks the potential of an already talented team; a single viral post that accelerates a brand's growth; an introduction between two people that sparks a billion-dollar partnership. In technology, the internet was a catalyst for countless industries — it didn't create value itself but dramatically accelerated value creation everywhere it touched.

---

## When to Use

- **Diagnosing slow progress:** When a process or goal is stalled, ask "What catalyst is missing?" rather than adding more raw effort.
- **Identifying leverage points:** In systems thinking, find the one element whose presence or absence dramatically changes system speed.
- **Team building:** Identify which people or roles will catalyze the performance of everyone around them.
- **Innovation strategy:** Look for technologies, partnerships, or policies that lower activation energy for your target outcome.
- **Personal growth:** Find the habits, mentors, or environments that accelerate your development without burning you out.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Mistaking activity for catalysis:** Not every input is a catalyst — adding more of something that isn't lowering barriers just adds cost.
- **Overestimating one catalyst:** A catalyst speeds a reaction but doesn't change the fundamental chemistry — if the underlying conditions are wrong, no catalyst will produce the desired outcome.
- **Ignoring inhibitors:** Focus on catalysts can blind you to inhibitors (things slowing the reaction) which may need to be removed first.
- **Assuming catalysts are permanent:** Catalysts can be deactivated (a mentor leaves, a technology becomes obsolete) — don't build a system entirely dependent on one.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Define the desired reaction:** Clearly state what outcome or transformation you want to accelerate.
2. **Identify what's slowing it:** What is the activation energy barrier — cost, skill gap, missing connection, regulatory hurdle?
3. **Search for existing catalysts:** Are there people, tools, relationships, or ideas that have historically accelerated this type of change?
4. **Assess fit:** Does the candidate catalyst specifically lower the relevant barrier, or is it general-purpose and unlikely to help?
5. **Apply the catalyst:** Introduce it with minimal disruption — catalysts work by lowering barriers, not by force.
6. **Monitor the reaction rate:** Measure whether the process is genuinely accelerating; distinguish catalyst effect from noise.
7. **Protect and preserve:** Ensure the catalyst isn't being consumed or degraded — if it is, find ways to sustain it.
8. **Iterate:** If one catalyst doesn't work, identify a different barrier and find the appropriate catalyst for it.
