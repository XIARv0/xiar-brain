# Counterinsurgency

## Description

**Counterinsurgency (COIN)** is the military doctrine for defeating an insurgency — an armed resistance movement embedded within a civilian population. The core insight, validated across historical cases, is that purely military suppression fails because it doesn't address the underlying grievances that sustain the insurgency. Winning requires "hearts and minds" — addressing the population's needs and legitimacy complaints that make them support or tolerate the insurgents. Applied broadly, it describes any strategy where the opposition derives strength from an underlying constituency whose grievances must be addressed, not merely suppressed.

- **Origins:** Classical COIN doctrine was developed through French experience in Algeria (David Galula's *Counterinsurgency Warfare*, 1964) and British experience in Malaya. The modern doctrine was codified in the U.S. Army's FM 3-24 field manual (2006), written under David Petraeus and James Mattis, which applied COIN principles to the Iraq War's Surge. The manual drew heavily on Galula's insight that "the population is the prize."

- **Key Principles:**
    - **The population is the prize:** An insurgency derives its power from the population — as recruits, informants, logistics support, and legitimacy. Whoever wins the population's loyalty, wins.
    - **Suppression without legitimacy fails:** Military force can eliminate individual insurgents but not the conditions that produce them. Kill the insurgent; create two more through resentment.
    - **Addressing grievances is strategy, not charity:** Providing security, governance, and basic services to the population is a military objective — not a humanitarian add-on. It removes the conditions that sustain the insurgency.
    - **Local knowledge is decisive:** Understanding the population's specific concerns, loyalties, and social structures is a decisive advantage. COIN requires deep local knowledge that conventional military operations don't need.
    - **Organizational application:** In any conflict where opposition draws strength from a constituency (discontented employees, alienated customers, resistant communities), the COIN model applies — suppress the most disruptive behavior while addressing the underlying grievances that sustain the opposition.

- **Real-World Examples:** The 2007 Iraq Surge succeeded partially because it combined military operations with political accommodation — providing Sunni tribes an alternative to Al-Qaeda's protection. A company that fires vocal critics without addressing the legitimate concerns driving their criticism creates more opposition. A leader who suppresses dissent without addressing the underlying team dysfunction finds resistance resurging.

---

## When to Use

- **Managing embedded organizational opposition:** When resistance to a change comes from a group with genuine grievances, suppression alone escalates the conflict.
- **Product and customer strategy:** When customer churn or public criticism is driven by specific unaddressed needs, fix the underlying problem rather than just managing the PR.
- **Community and stakeholder relations:** When a project faces sustained opposition from a community, identify and address the legitimate concerns before attempting political suppression.
- **Labor relations:** Persistent labor unrest signals underlying conditions — address them structurally, not just through enforcement.

---

## When to Avoid

- **Pure bad-faith opposition:** Some opposition is not grievance-based but ideologically opposed to your existence. COIN-style accommodation doesn't work against existential opponents with no legitimate grievances.
- **When speed is critical:** Addressing underlying grievances takes time. In fast-moving crises requiring immediate action, suppression may be necessary to create the stability in which COIN principles can then be applied.

---

## Thinking Steps

1. **Identify the opposition and its constituency:** Who is opposing you? Who supports or tolerates them? What sustains the opposition?
2. **Separate behavior from grievances:** What specific actions must be stopped? What underlying conditions make people willing to support or tolerate those actions?
3. **Assess legitimacy of the grievances:** Are the underlying concerns valid? Often they are — even if the opposition's methods are not.
4. **Design a two-track response:** Address the most disruptive behavior directly while simultaneously addressing the underlying grievances.
5. **Prioritize the population's (constituency's) needs:** What do the people who sustain the opposition most need? Provide it — not as charity but as strategy.
6. **Build local legitimacy:** Demonstrate that you can meet the population's needs better than the opposition. Legitimacy, not force, is the decisive long-term factor.
7. **Measure success by the population, not the opponent:** Progress is measured by whether the constituency's support for the opposition is declining, not by the number of opponents suppressed.
